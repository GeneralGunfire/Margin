Covers the LEGACY five-topic Gr 11 (default assumption). The DRAFT amended CAPS topics (Atlantic World, slavery and Haiti, southern African kingdoms, Scramble for Africa) are NOT given depth until the school confirms; see history.md. Dates and facts below are standard well-established history; anything I am less sure of is marked UNVERIFIED. No sources or exam questions are copied. Resources: none verified yet (Pass 3). Each topic has an "essay vs source-based" angle as in the 2023/24 ATP.

## Skills spine (all topics; Term 1 start)
- Source skills: extraction (quote/use evidence), define terms, interpret/explain, compare sources, usefulness (purpose, author, date, audience), reliability, bias, limitations. Cognitive split Gr 11: 30% L1, 50% L2, 20% L3.
- Essay skills: read the question, plan, introduction that answers the question, PEEL paragraphs, line of argument, conclusion linked to introduction; chronology.
- Mistakes: narrating without answering; copying the source; judging usefulness without a reason; no balance.
- Lessons: L1 "Reading a source"; L2 "Usefulness and reliability"; L3 "Essay planning"; L4 "PEEL paragraphs".
- Visuals: source-analysis template; essay planning frame; timelines.

## Topic 1: Communism in Russia 1900-1940 (Paper 1) — rebuilt from the Via Afrika guide, pp. 2-42
Source: Via Afrika Gr 11 History Study Guide (see resources-history-via-afrika.md). Key question the guide poses: how was communism applied in Russia under Lenin and Stalin. Dates are the guide's unless flagged. "CHECK" marks a date or claim that disagrees with standard references (see the date-check table in resources-history-via-afrika.md).

### Unit 1 (guide pp. 3-8): Communism, Marx, 1905
- **Key ideas:** socialism vs communism (communal ownership, no classes or state in principle); Marx (born 1818): Communist Manifesto 1848 (with Engels), Das Kapital 1867; bourgeoisie vs proletariat, class struggle, expected workers' revolution.
- **Russia before 1905:** autocratic Tsar, multinational empire, mostly peasants; serfs freed 1861 (Alexander II); rapid industrialisation after 1860; Alexander II killed 1881 and reforms stopped under Alexander III; Marxist ideas spread in the 1890s; the party split in 1903 into Bolsheviks (Lenin, small disciplined elite party) and Mensheviks. CHECK: the guide says the Social-Democratic Party formed in "1861"; the standard date is 1898.
- **1905:** defeat by Japan (war from Feb 1904), then Bloody Sunday (massacre of a peaceful petition march to the Winter Palace, led by Father Gapon), strikes and mutinies, October Manifesto (civil rights, a Duma), December Moscow uprising crushed, Fundamental Laws leave the Tsar a veto. CHECK: the guide says the revolution broke out "February 1905"; Bloody Sunday was 22 January 1905 (9 January old style).
- **Link 1905 to 1917:** soviets (workers' councils) tried out; Trotsky chaired the St Petersburg Soviet; Marxists learned the power of mass strikes; theory revised to allow a socialist revolution in backward Russia (Trotsky's "permanent revolution").
- **Skills practised:** timeline, cause and effect, interpreting a Marx extract, defining terms (Duma, zemstvo, soviet, Bolshevik).

### Unit 2 (pp. 9-21): February and October 1917
- **Calendar note (useful and correct):** Russia used the Julian calendar, 13 days behind, until February 1918, hence "February/March" and "October/November".
- **Causes (political, economic, social):** Tsar refused real reform after 1905 (Stolypin PM 1906-11, repressive, assassinated Sept 1911); Tsar took army command in 1915 and Tsarina Alexandra with Rasputin ran the government; WWI defeats, food and fuel shortages, inflation, strikes, overcrowded housing in Petrograd.
- **February revolution:** unplanned; International Women's Day protests grew into a mass strike; troops joined the crowds; Duma formed a Provisional Government; Tsar abdicated (guide: 14 March; CHECK, standard 15 March new style / 2 March old style). The guide's "by 28 October 1917 over 80 000 troops mutinied" sits in the February narrative and is almost certainly a slip; treat as CHECK.
- **Provisional Government failure:** Petrograd Soviet had equal power (dual power); Order Number 1 undermined army discipline; no land reform, war continued, elections postponed. The guide names Kerensky as leader from the start; standard accounts have Prince Lvov first, Kerensky from July (the guide's own question 1.10 lists Lvov).
- **Lenin's return (April 1917) and April Theses:** "Peace, Land, Bread", all power to the soviets; July Days (Bolsheviks blamed, Lenin hid, Trotsky jailed); Kornilov affair (August) boosted the Bolsheviks; Bolshevik majorities in Petrograd and Moscow soviets by September.
- **October revolution:** planned; Trotsky's Military-Revolutionary Committee; key buildings taken 24-25 Oct (old style), Winter Palace taken, cruiser Aurora; decrees on land and peace; Constituent Assembly met once in January 1918 and was dissolved; Brest-Litovsk (March 1918) took Russia out of WWI; Cheka set up.
- **People:** Nicholas II, Alexandra, Rasputin, Stolypin, Kerensky, Lvov, Kornilov, Lenin, Trotsky, Stalin (Commissar of Nationalities), Gapon. CHECK: the guide says "the Tsar had Rasputin killed in 1916"; he was killed by nobles (Dec 1916).
- **Skills practised:** causes and consequences, sequencing, comparing the two revolutions (spontaneous vs planned), reading the 1917 proclamation and the Rodzianko telegram as sources.
- **Essay angles:** why did the Provisional Government fail; why could the Bolsheviks take power; to what extent was October a coup.

### Unit 3 (pp. 22-28): Civil war, War Communism, NEP, Lenin's Marxism, Lenin's death
- **Civil war 1918-21:** Reds (Trotsky) vs Whites with Western backing; Cheka terror; Tsar's family killed 1918; Red victory helped by White disunity.
- **War Communism:** nationalised industry, grain requisitioning, labour conscription, banned private trade; result: economic collapse, 1921 famine (guide: five million died), Kronstadt mutiny (March 1921) crushed.
- **NEP (1921):** a compromise: grain tax replaced requisitioning; small private trade and factories allowed (Nepmen, kulaks); state kept banks and heavy industry; output recovered (1926 grain near 1913 level) but unemployment and poor transport remained; some Bolsheviks saw it as a betrayal of Marxism.
- **Lenin's interpretation of Marxism:** revolution possible in a peasant country; elite vanguard party; dictatorship of the proletariat; one-party state; USSR name (guide: 1923; CHECK, formed December 1922). CHECK: the guide's summary says the revolutionaries overthrew the Tsar and Provisional Government "in 1921"; it was 1917.
- **Women:** February protests; Provisional Government gave the vote; Bolsheviks gave legal equality, divorce on request, Zhenotdel; later rolled back under Stalin.
- **Lenin's death (1924):** Trotsky (internationalism, permanent revolution) vs Stalin (Socialism in One Country); the troika (Stalin, Kamenev, Zinoviev) isolated Trotsky; Stalin then removed the others; Trotsky exiled and later murdered (1940; the guide gives no date).
- **Key terms:** vanguard, dictatorship of the proletariat, War Communism, NEP, kulak, Nepmen, Cheka, Zhenotdel, troika.
- **Skills practised:** compare War Communism, NEP and the Five-Year Plans in a table (guide Q6); evidence on Kronstadt.

### Unit 4 (pp. 29-36): Russia under Stalin
- **Stalin's interpretation:** Socialism in One Country, economic modernisation before world revolution, strong bureaucracy.
- **Collectivisation from 1929:** kolkhoz (collective) vs sovkhoz (state farm); kulaks resisted (burned crops, killed livestock), arrested or sent to gulags; famine 1930 and 1932-33 (guide: 6 million; estimates in the literature vary, so present as an estimate); by 1941 almost all land collectivised.
- **Five-Year Plans:** Gosplan; First 1928-33 (heavy industry), Second 1933-37, Third 1938-41 (interrupted by war); industrial superpower but poor consumer goods, housing shortage, forced labour.
- **Terror:** Kirov murdered 1934; show trials 1934-38 (Kamenev and Zinoviev executed 1936); NKVD head Yezhov from 1936, later replaced; Red Army purge from 1937; the guide says 20 million sent to gulags with half dying (guide's estimate; flag as uncertain); censorship, cult of personality, apparatchiks.
- **Women under Stalin:** more education and work (28% of industrial workers in 1927, 40% in 1937) but abortion banned, divorce harder.
- **WWII:** Molotov-Ribbentrop pact 1939; Operation Barbarossa 22 June 1941. CHECK: the p. 39 summary says Stalin "broke pact with Nazi Germany in 1942"; Germany broke it in 1941 (the guide contradicts itself).
- **Skills practised:** analyse a propaganda poster (guide Q7), compare policies in a table, evidence for and against "success" of the plans.
- **Essay angles:** how far were the Five-Year Plans a success; was Stalinism a continuation of Leninism; why did Stalin win the succession struggle.

### Common mistakes (Topic 1)
Mixing Marx, Lenin and Stalin; using the 13-day calendar difference carelessly; treating February and October as one event; saying NEP ended communism instead of being a temporary compromise; quoting casualty figures as exact.

### Lesson breakdown (Topic 1)
L1 Marx, communism and the 1905 revolution (pp. 3-8); L2 February 1917 and the Provisional Government (pp. 9-18); L3 Lenin, October 1917 and the Bolsheviks in power (pp. 16-21); L4 Civil war, War Communism, NEP and Lenin's death (pp. 22-28); L5 Stalin: collectivisation, plans and terror (pp. 29-36); summary and questions on pp. 37-42.

### Guide features useful for Margin
Flow-chart style summaries per unit; end-of-topic summary; question types: matching leaders, defining terms, place-name gap-fill, comparison table, poster analysis. Answers from p. 195.

## Topic 2: Capitalism in the USA 1900-1940 (Paper 1) — rebuilt from the Via Afrika guide, pp. 43-76
Key question in the guide: how did the Great Depression bring about a crisis of capitalism. "CHECK" = disagrees with standard references.

### Unit 1 (pp. 44-54): Nature of capitalism, the American Dream, the 1920s boom
- **Background:** USA overtook Britain and Germany in industrial output early 1900s; WWI made it a supplier and lender to the Allies; entered the war in 1917 after German unrestricted submarine warfare; Wilson (1913-21) helped design the League of Nations but the Senate did not ratify Versailles, so the USA stayed out and turned isolationist.
- **1920s politics:** Harding (1920, "return to normalcy"), Coolidge from 1923/24 ("the business of America is business"); laissez-faire, tax cuts favouring the rich, high tariffs, anti-union action; Immigration Act 1924 (quotas, cut southern/eastern Europe).
- **American Dream:** rags-to-riches, rugged individualism; reality of class, race and ethnic inequality.
- **Boom drivers:** mass production (Model T "Tin Lizzie"), advertising and hire purchase/credit, rising share prices, buying shares on the margin (about 10% down).
- **Weak spots under the boom:** tariffs provoked foreign retaliation; wages lagged behind profits; monopolies (e.g. US Steel); agricultural overproduction and falling farm prices (about 6 million pushed off farms per the guide); top 5% took about a third of income (guide's figure, source not given).
- **Society in the "Roaring Twenties":** jazz, radio, flappers, women's vote but little power, rising divorce; African American Great Migration, Harlem, Jim Crow in the South, Ku Klux Klan (about 5 million members mid-1920s per guide); Prohibition (1920-33), bootlegging, speakeasies, Al Capone. CHECK: the guide says "1931: end of Depression, states realised liquor tax would bring revenue"; the Depression did not end in 1931 (likely meant the Depression's depth was clear).
- **Skills practised:** cause and effect (boom causes vs hidden weaknesses), cartoons/photographs of the 1920s, comparing who benefited (guide Q1 mind map).

### Unit 2 (pp. 55-58): Wall Street Crash 1929
- **Events:** 24 October 1929 (panic selling, 13 million shares), Black Tuesday 29 October.
- **Causes:** speculation and margin buying; weak banks lending to speculators; overproduction and falling spending power; uneven wealth; tariffs and collapsing trade; laissez-faire (no regulation).
- **Impact:** output about 30% down, prices 25% down, national income halved by 1933 (guide); bank failures (650 in 1929, 4 000 in 1933 per guide); unemployment, evictions, Hoovervilles, breadlines, food riots, Bonus Marchers; no social security; Hoover's late and limited measures.
- **Skills practised:** interpreting graphs and photographs, explaining "causes vs trigger".

### Unit 3 (pp. 59-66): The New Deal
- **Why FDR won (1932):** anger at Hoover; promised a "New Deal for the forgotten man"; Brains Trust; first 100 days (15 major laws); fireside chats from 1933.
- **Three Rs:** relief, recovery, reform.
- **First New Deal (1933-mid 1935):** Emergency Banking Relief Act (bank holiday, deposit guarantee), FERA, alphabet agencies, NIRA with PWA and NRA ("Blue Eagle"), NRA ruled unconstitutional 1935.
- **Second New Deal (1935-40):** WPA (over 3 million jobs by 1938), Social Security Act 1935, Wagner Act 1935 (collective bargaining), "soak the rich" tax.
- **Opposition:** wealthy and business (taxes, unions, "socialism"), conservative Supreme Court (NRA 1935, AAA 1936), Huey Long, Father Coughlin, communists, Southern Democrats (racism; 1936 walkout).
- **Weaken or strengthen capitalism?** Strengthened: restored confidence, regulated abuses, saved capitalism from revolution; weakened (according to critics): bigger state, big labour and big government at the table; not a cure: 1937-38 recession after spending cuts; excluded many African Americans and women.
- **Essay angles:** "To what extent did the New Deal strengthen capitalism?"; "Was the New Deal a success?"; compare Hoover and FDR.

### Unit 4 (pp. 67-72): WWII and the world
- **Recovery:** 1937 spending cuts caused a new slump (guide: 5 million lost jobs, CHECK: figures in standard sources are lower, quote with care); Neutrality Acts 1935-37; rearmament from 1938/39, drafting 1940, Pearl Harbor December 1941 ended the slump.
- **Global impact:** USSR (cut off from the world economy, claimed immunity), Latin America (export collapse), Germany (loans recalled, bank collapse, Nazis and communists gained), Japan (military control, deficit spending, recovery), South Africa (off the gold standard in 1932, gold boom, Afrikaner poor-white problem, Carnegie Commission on Poor Whites, link to Afrikaner nationalism).
- **Conclusion:** the boom-bust cycle; the lesson that some state regulation was needed.
- **Skills practised:** link to Topic 1 (USSR avoiding the Depression) and Topic 4 (SA effects), evaluating sources on the global crisis.

### Common mistakes (Topic 2)
Treating the Crash as the sole cause of the Depression; mixing Hoover and FDR policies; saying the New Deal ended the Depression; leaving out the 1937-38 recession; omitting African Americans and women from the "who benefited" question.

### Lesson breakdown (Topic 2)
L1 American capitalism and the 1920s boom (pp. 44-50); L2 Society in the 1920s: women, race, Prohibition (pp. 51-54); L3 The Crash and the Depression (pp. 55-58); L4 FDR and the New Deal (pp. 59-66); L5 WWII and global impact, essay practice (pp. 67-72).

### Guide features useful for Margin
Summary box per unit; questions: mind maps of causes and consequences, define terms in paragraph form (start/end date, background, outcome, meaning), complete a New Deal agency diagram (relief, recovery, reform), answers from p. 195.

## Topic 3: Ideas of race in the late 19th and 20th centuries (Paper 1) — rebuilt from the Via Afrika guide, pp. 77-100
Key question in the guide: what were the consequences when pseudo-scientific ideas of race became part of government policy and law. The guide covers BOTH case studies (Australia and Nazi Germany); CAPS asks for one of the two. "CHECK" = disagrees with standard references.

### Unit 1 (pp. 78-82): Theories and practice of race
- **Race science:** Linnaeus (1730s, four groups), Blumenbach (1795, five groups, Caucasian "best"), Camper (1790s, facial/skull ranking), Samuel Morton (mid-1800s, brain size; the guide's unit text writes "Stanley Morton", CHECK, its summary writes Samuel), Tiedemann disagreed, Nott and Gliddon (1854, table of races).
- **Darwin and Social Darwinism:** natural selection; Herbert Spencer ("survival of the fittest"); used to justify imperial rule, racial hierarchies and human exhibitions (e.g. Saartjie Baartman).
- **Eugenics:** Francis Galton coined the word; Davenport's Eugenics Record Office; positive eugenics (better-baby and fittest-family contests) vs negative eugenics (sterilisation: 30 US states, about 65 000 people per the guide).
- **Modern understanding:** all humans one species; Human Genome Project (guide: "2008", and "in 2003" in the next line; CHECK, the project ran 1990-2003) showed about 99.9% genetic sameness; race has no biological basis.
- **Practices by place:** USA (marriage laws, IQ tests, immigration limits, Native Americans); Australia (assimilation, "breeding out"); Germany (race purity); South Africa (anti-miscegenation, segregation, birth-control for white supremacy); Namibia (1904 genocide of the Herero and Nama, Eugen Fischer's research that later influenced the Nazis).
- **Skills practised:** explaining a "scientific" claim and why it is pseudo-science; comparing practices across countries; source work on eugenics posters.

### Case study 1: Australia and indigenous Australians (pp. 83-86)
- **Background:** British colony at Botany Bay/Port Jackson 26 January 1788; convicts (162 000 to 1867 per guide); conflict and dispossession; smallpox; population fell (guide: about 1 million to about 80 000 by 1888, treat as an estimate); colonies and federation in 1901 (the guide writes South Australia "1936", CHECK: 1836).
- **Race theories and policy:** Aboriginal people seen as "primitive"; eugenics societies; "racial decay/suicide" fears; Immigration Restriction Act 1901 (White Australia policy); £5 baby bonus 1912; British child migrants (1920-67, the "Lost Generation").
- **Stolen Generations:** assimilation policy to "breed out" Aboriginal ancestry (Spencer 1913 proposals, Cook and Neville in the 1930s, Hasluck 1951); about 100 000 children removed 1910-70 (guide's figure, estimates differ); policy ended in 1971 per the guide; official inquiry 1997; apology by PM Rudd (guide: 2009; CHECK: the apology to the Stolen Generations was 13 February 2008) and a later apology for the Lost Generation (guide: 2010).
- **Skills practised:** evaluating government policy documents and photographs; empathy and perspective in sources from different sides; essay on "how did race theories shape Australian policy".

### Case study 2: Nazi Germany and the Holocaust (pp. 87-94)
- **Consolidating power 1933-34:** Hitler chancellor 1933; Reichstag Fire decree; Enabling Act; banning other parties and unions; Night of the Long Knives 30 June 1934 (guide: about 400 killed; estimates vary); Hindenburg's death 1934 and the title Führer; SS and Himmler, Gestapo.
- **Racial ideology:** Aryan master race (Herrenvolk), Social Darwinism and eugenics, Fischer, Bauer, Lenz; "the other" as scapegoat.
- **Positive eugenics:** Lebensborn (guide: 1936; CHECK, usually dated 1935). **Negative eugenics:** Sterilisation Law July 1933 (300 000-400 000 per guide), concentration camps for "asocials", Operation T4 euthanasia (70 273 deaths by gassing per Nazi records).
- **Targeted groups:** Jews (boycotts, Nuremberg Laws 1935, Aryanisation, Kristallnacht November 1938, curfews and confiscation 1939); Sinti and Roma; political and religious opponents; "asocials", homosexuals, disabled people.
- **Choices people made:** perpetrators, bystanders, resisters, rescuers (e.g. Le Chambon); Jewish responses: exile, accommodation, defiance (Warsaw Ghetto uprising April 1943).
- **Final Solution:** Einsatzgruppen, ghettos, Chelmno gas vans, death camps (Auschwitz, Treblinka, Majdanek), Zyklon-B, about 6 million Jews killed; death marches; liberation of Auschwitz January 1945 (Soviets) and Bergen-Belsen (British).
- **Justice:** Nuremberg trials (retributive justice), shortcomings, link to later restorative justice such as South Africa's TRC.
- **Sensitive content:** handle Holocaust and genocide material with care; show propaganda sources with context, not as endorsement (flag for the Margin content team).
- **Skills practised:** analysing Nazi propaganda illustrations (guide Q2-4: symbols, audience, purpose), identifying bias, comparing perpetrators and bystanders.
- **Essay angles:** how did eugenics shape Nazi policy; to what extent was the Holocaust inevitable once the Nazis took power; why did most Germans remain bystanders.

### Common mistakes (Topic 3)
Treating race as biological; presenting racist claims as fact; blurring the Nuremberg Laws (1935), Kristallnacht (1938) and the Final Solution (1941-42); giving casualty figures as exact; confusing the Warsaw Ghetto uprising (1943) with the 1944 Warsaw Uprising (the guide's Q1 g says 1944; CHECK).

### Lesson breakdown (Topic 3)
L1 Race science and Social Darwinism (pp. 78-79); L2 Eugenics and its practice in USA, SA, Namibia (pp. 80-82); L3 Australia: colonisation to the Stolen Generations (pp. 83-86); L4 Nazi consolidation and racial ideology (pp. 87-90); L5 Persecution to Final Solution and justice (pp. 90-94), with the summary and propaganda-source questions (pp. 95-100).

### Guide features useful for Margin
Explicit "perpetrator, bystander, resister, rescuer" frame; summary boxes; propaganda source activities with scaffolded questions (symbol, audience, message); answers from p. 195.

## Topic 4: Nationalisms — South Africa, the Middle East and Africa (Paper 2) — rebuilt from the Via Afrika guide, pp. 101-164
Key question in the guide: when is nationalism beneficial and when is it destructive. The guide treats four case studies (African nationalism in SA, Afrikaner nationalism in SA, the Middle East, Gold Coast to Ghana); my earlier notes said schools choose two case studies, so which two the school teaches is UNVERIFIED. "CHECK" = disagrees with standard references.

### Unit 1 (pp. 102-106): What is nationalism?
- **Idea:** loyalty to a nation with a shared identity; modern nationalism emerged in Western Europe (1700s-1800s) as new social classes challenged feudal monarchs; French Revolution 1789 (will of the majority), Napoleonic rule 1795-1815 sparked resistance, Congress of Vienna 1815 tried to restore the old order and stimulated nationalism.
- **Spread:** revolutions of 1830 (France July revolution, Belgium independent 1832, Greece, Polish uprising) and 1848; unification of Germany (Zollverein, Bismarck, three wars) and Italy (Cavour, Garibaldi).
- **Industry, middle class and imperialism:** Industrial Revolution created a middle class that backed nationalism; New Imperialism; Berlin Conference 1884-85 split Africa (the guide calls it the "Congress of Berlin" in one place; CHECK: that name usually means 1878); colonial borders laid the base for African nationalism.
- **Imagined community:** Benedict Anderson (1983): a nation is a socially constructed community held together by shared symbols, language and print media. Reused in the Afrikaner case study.
- **Skills practised:** define nationalism in own words, apply the theory to an example, compare European and African nationalism.

### Case study 1a (pp. 107-122): The rise of African nationalism in South Africa
- **Context:** Union of 1910 gave power to whites; Natives Land Act 1913; migrant labour; 1920s-40s forced urbanisation.
- **Early organisations:** APO (Dr Abdurahman, coloured; London delegation 1909), Natal Indian Congress (1894, Gandhi; passive resistance 1908), SANNC (8 January 1912, Bloemfontein; renamed ANC 1924; "politics of petition"; newspaper Abantu Batho), ICU (1919, Kadalie; faded by 1930), Bantu Women's League and anti-pass protests (1913, 1918), 1920 miners' strike (about 70 000), SACP and trade unions.
- **Two strands:** non-racial inclusive nationalism (Xuma, communists) vs Africanist (Lembede, Youth League, later Sobukwe's PAC).
- **WWII influences:** Atlantic Charter 1941, Xuma's African Claims (1943), returning soldiers, Manchester Pan-African Congress 1945. CHECK: the guide says the Manchester Congress "gave rise to" the 1943 African Claims, which is out of chronological order. 1946 mineworkers' strike (over 60 000; guide: 12 killed).
- **From ANC Youth League to Congress Alliance:** Youth League 1944 (Lembede, Mandela, Sisulu, Tambo), Programme of Action, Moroka replaced Xuma in 1949, Congress of the People and Freedom Charter (Kliptown, 1955), PAC breakaway 1959 (Sobukwe; anti-pass campaign leading to Sharpeville and Langa).
- **Later tradition:** Black Consciousness (Biko), Morogoro conference 1969, Soweto 1976, UDF launched 20 August 1983 (Rocklands, Mitchell's Plain).
- **Nation-building:** FW de Klerk president 1989, Mandela released 11 February 1990, CODESA (December 1991), first non-racial elections 27 April 1994, GNU, 1995 Rugby World Cup, Constitution (Act 108 of 1996), TRC (Act 34 of 1995, Archbishop Tutu).
- **Skills practised:** extract and compare evidence from Plaatje's 1918 account and ANC documents, causes-and-consequences, judging inclusive vs exclusive nationalism.
- **Essay angles:** why did African nationalism take about forty years to become a mass movement; how did WWII change it; how did inclusive nationalism help reconciliation.

### Case study 1b (pp. 123-134): The rise of Afrikaner nationalism
- **Roots:** Great Trek (1835-46) and Boer republics, "chosen people" idea; Stephanus du Toit and the GRA (Paarl, 14 August 1875, Die Afrikaanse Patriot); Afrikaner Bond.
- **Drivers:** economic (English-dominated economy), poor whites and the Great Depression, culture.
- **Movements:** First Language Movement 1875-1900, Second Language Movement 1900-1930 (anglicisation under Milner, Afrikaans recognised 1925, Bible 1933); Afrikaner Broederbond (Jong Zuid Afrika 1918, Broederbond 1919); FAK cultural bodies; Christian National Trade Unions; Volkskapitalisme (Reddingsdaadbond; Volkskongres 1939); newspapers Die Burger, Die Transvaler, Die Huisgenoot.
- **Volk ideas:** Kuyper's "spheres"; Volk defined as white Afrikaans speakers; links to education (Christian National Education 1939, later Bantu Education), labour (colour bar; 1911 Mines and Works Act; Hertzog's "civilised labour" policy) and the Dutch Reformed Church (volksmoeders, segregation as church duty).
- **1930s-48:** 1934 split of Malan's "purified" National Party from Hertzog and Smuts; Eeufees 1938 (ox-wagon trek, Voortrekker Monument stone laid 16 December 1938); 1948 election: NP used swart gevaar rhetoric, narrowly won, started apartheid.
- **Afrikaner nationalism today:** little left of the old project, small pockets such as Orania.
- **Skills practised:** link to Imagined Communities (Topic 4 Unit 1), analyse the 1939 DRC and Huisgenoot extracts, compare with African nationalism (guide Q4).

### Case study 2 (pp. 135-148): The Middle East
- **Origins:** Jews (diaspora, Zionism from the 1890s, Herzl, Basle 1897); Arabs under Ottoman rule, First Arab National Congress 1913; WWI promises vs the Sykes-Picot Treaty and the Balfour Declaration (1917); League of Nations mandates 1920s.
- **Palestine under Britain:** more Jewish immigration, Arab protests 1921 and 1929, Haganah, 1936 Arab revolt, 1937 partition proposal, 1930s surge of Jewish refugees.
- **1947-49:** UN Partition Plan (guide: more than half to the Jewish state), civil war, Ben-Gurion declared Israel 14 May 1948, invasion by Arab states, 1949 armistices; two perspectives (Israeli "war of independence" vs Palestinian An-Nakba, UN Resolution 194 on refugees).
- **Arab nationalism and the region:** Arab League 1945; Nasser, Suez 1956 (guide's table lists 1952-56; the coup was 1952, nationalisation 1956); United Arab Republic 1958-61; PLO 1964 (the guide says Arafat was first leader; CHECK: Ahmad Shukeiri was first chairman, Arafat from 1969).
- **Wars:** Six-Day War June 1967 (UN Resolution 242), Yom Kippur War 1973, first intifada 1987-93; Camp David (guide: 1979 treaty), PLO recognised Israel 1988, Oslo Accords 1993 (guide says signed "in Oslo, Sweden"; CHECK: negotiated in Oslo, Norway, signed in Washington), second intifada from 2000; the guide ends "no peaceful solution in sight" (an opinion, flag for neutral wording).
- **Sensitive content:** multi-perspective and politically sensitive; present both accounts, do not endorse either.
- **Skills practised:** comparing two accounts (guide's two-column table), assessing causes of 1948, map and timeline work.
- **Essay angles:** to what extent did Britain's promises cause conflict; why has peace failed; compare Israeli and Palestinian nationalism.

### Case study 3 (pp. 149-155): From Gold Coast to Ghana
- **Colonial impact:** British colony by 1901, educated coastal elite, cocoa farmers, strengthened chiefs; NCBWA (founded 1919, first conference Accra March 1920) and its elitist bias; Garvey's influence; cocoa boycott 1937-38; trade unions.
- **WWII:** Atlantic Charter, soldiers, 1948 ex-servicemen march and riots in Accra (guide: 29 killed); UGCC 1947 (Danquah).
- **Nkrumah:** studied in the USA; Pan-African Congress Manchester 1945; UGCC general secretary 1947; formed the CPP June 1949; Positive Action January 1950; prime minister 1952; independence 6 March 1957 (guide: March 1957); republic 1960; detention without trial 1958; economic failures; coup 1966; died 1972.
- **Positive and negative:** unifying national struggle, then authoritarian rule, personality cult, debt.
- **Skills practised:** causes of independence, evaluating Nkrumah's legacy, comparing with SA nationalism.

### Unit 2 (pp. 156-157): Positive and negative features of nationalism
Inclusive (SA after 1994, Ghana before independence) vs exclusive (Afrikaner nationalism, Nazism, Zionism/Israel-Palestine tension in the guide's wording); post-1990 Eastern Europe and Bosnia 1992 as a destructive example (the guide says communism collapsed in the USSR "in 1990"; CHECK: 1991).

### Common mistakes (Topic 4)
Confusing African and Afrikaner nationalism; giving one perspective on the Middle East; dating the ANC and PAC splits wrongly (Youth League 1944, PAC 1959); confusing the African Claims (1943) with Manchester (1945); treating nationalism as only good or only bad.

### Lesson breakdown (Topic 4)
Core: L1 What is nationalism (pp. 102-106). Per case study: L1 context and origins; L2 organisations and leaders; L3 turning points; L4 essay and source practice. Guide pages: 1a pp. 109-122 (five lessons suit it), 1b pp. 123-134, Middle East pp. 135-148, Ghana pp. 149-155, positives and negatives pp. 156-157.

### Guide features useful for Margin
