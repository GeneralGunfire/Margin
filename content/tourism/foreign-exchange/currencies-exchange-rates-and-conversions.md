## The idea in plain language

When a British tourist lands in Cape Town carrying **Pounds (`GBP`)**, hotels and game reserves cannot accept Pounds at the till — they need **South African Rand (`ZAR`)** to pay local staff and suppliers.

An **exchange rate** simply tells you **how many Rand (`ZAR`) one unit of a foreign currency costs** (`for example, 1 USD = R18.45`). Let's test live conversions with our interactive calculator before locking down the golden rules!

## Part 1: Major Currencies, Codes & Where to Exchange

| Currency Name | 3-Letter ISO Code | Symbol | Origin |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **South African Rand** | **ZAR** | **R** | South Africa (`local currency`) |
| **United States Dollar** | **USD** | **$** | United States (`major global benchmark`) |
| **Euro** | **EUR** | **€** | Eurozone countries (`France, Germany, Italy, Spain, etc.`) |
| **British Pound Sterling** | **GBP** | **£** | United Kingdom |
| **Japanese Yen** | **JPY** | **¥** | Japan (`notice 1 JPY is worth ~R0.12 — tiny unit!`) |
| **Australian Dollar** | **AUD** | **A$** | Australia |

- **Where to exchange:** **Commercial banks** (`e.g. FNB, Standard Bank, Absa, Nedbank`) and licensed **Foreign Exchange Bureaux (`Bureaux de Change`, e.g. Travelex at OR Tambo/CPT)**, plus international card ATMs and travel-wallet cards.
- **Why Exchange Rates Fluctuate:** Political stability, inflation, interest rates, investor confidence, commodity prices, and tourism demand move currency prices daily!
  - **Weak Rand (`e.g. 1 USD rises from R15 to R19`):** **Great for INBOUND tourists** (*South Africa feels much cheaper — hotels, safaris, dining cost fewer Dollars/Euros!*), **expensive for OUTBOUND South Africans** travelling abroad!
  - **Strong Rand (`e.g. 1 USD drops from R19 to R15`):** **Great for OUTBOUND South Africans** travelling overseas (`cheaper flights/hotels in London`), **more expensive for INBOUND visitors**.

## Part 2: The Golden Conversion Rules (`Read Exam Tables Carefully!`)

In Grade 11 exam rate sheets, rates are normally printed as **Rand per 1 unit of foreign currency** (`e.g. 1 EUR = R19.95`):

```steps
title: How to NEVER confuse multiply vs divide in Grade 11 Forex
step: **1. Identify which rate column the paper gives:** Check that the header says **ZAR per 1 unit of currency** (`if a question ever gives foreign per R1 instead, check carefully!`). Using standard **1 Foreign = R(rate)**:
step: **2. Converting FOREIGN -> RAND (`ZAR`):** **MULTIPLY** by the rate!
$$\text{Amount in ZAR} = \text{Foreign Amount} \times \text{Exchange Rate}$$
step: **3. Converting RAND (`ZAR`) -> FOREIGN:** **DIVIDE** by the rate!
$$\text{Foreign Amount} = \text{Amount in ZAR} \div \text{Exchange Rate}$$
step: **4. Round ONLY at the very end to TWO decimal places:** Never round mid-calculation! Show the **currency code/symbol (`R` or `GBP / USD`)** in your final answer.
```

## Part 3: The Tourism Multiplier Effect (`Why Inbound Forex Matters`)

Why does **R10 000** spent by an Australian tourist boost the town by *far more* than R10 000? Because money **circulates through layers**:
1. **Direct spend:** Tourist pays **R10 000** to a Kruger lodge for rooms and meals.
2. **Indirect spend:** The lodge pays local **farmers** for fresh vegetables, a **laundry service**, a **fuel station**, and **guided-tour contractors**.
3. **Induced spend:** Waiters, guides, and farm workers spend their wages at **local supermarkets, schools, pharmacies, and hair salons**!
- *(Note **leakage**: if the lodge imports foreign wine or sends profits overseas, part of the multiplier leaks out of South Africa!)*

## Worked example: Exam-Style Rate Sheet Conversions

Use this rate sheet to answer both questions (**round to 2 decimal places**):
- `1 USD = R18.45`
- `1 GBP = R23.40`
- `1 JPY = R0.12`

1. **Foreign -> ZAR:** *A British tourist pays a safari lodge **`GBP 420`** in Rand equivalent. Calculate the Rand value.*
   $$\text{ZAR} = 420 \times 23.40 = \mathbf{R9\,828.00}$$

2. **ZAR -> Foreign:** *A South African student flying to Tokyo exchanges **`R4 500`** into Japanese Yen (`JPY`). How many Yen do they receive?*
   $$\text{JPY} = 4\,500 \div 0.12 = \mathbf{JPY\ 37\,500.00}$$

3. **Comparing two currencies:** *A souvenir jacket costs `R2 214.00`. How much is that in `USD`?*
   $$\text{USD} = 2\,214.00 \div 18.45 = \mathbf{USD\ 120.00}$$

2. Convert **R3 025.00** to Australian Dollars (`AUD`).

> **Common mistakes**
>
> - Multiplying `R4 500 × 0.12` instead of dividing when converting **ZAR -> JPY**, giving a tiny impossible number (`540 JPY`!) instead of `37 500.00 JPY`! Always sanity-check whether `1 unit of foreign currency` is worth *more* or *less* than `R1`.
> - Leaving off **two decimal places** (`write R3 591.00, not R3 591`) or omitting the currency code (`USD`, `EUR`, `R`).
> - Saying a **"weak Rand is bad for all tourism"** — a weak Rand actually **encourages inbound tourists** because South Africa gives them more Rand per Dollar/Euro!

## Practice

```questions
Q: Using `1 USD = R18.45` and `1 GBP = R23.40`, calculate: *(a)* **USD 350** in Rand; *(b)* **R5 850** in British Pounds (`round to 2 decimal places`). (4)
A: *(a)* `350 × 18.45 =` **`R6 457.50`**. *(b)* `5 850 ÷ 23.40 =` **`GBP 250.00`**.
---
Q: Explain what happens to **inbound international tourism to South Africa** when the Rand weakens significantly against the Euro (`EUR`), and give ONE reason. (3)
A: **Inbound European arrivals normally increase** because each Euro buys **more South African Rand**, making accommodation, safaris, dining, and gifts look much more affordable to European visitors.
---
Q: Briefly explain the **tourism multiplier effect** using a guesthouse as an example. (4)
A: A tourist's direct payment to a guesthouse circulates repeatedly through the local economy: the guesthouse pays local **suppliers** (`bakeries, laundries, guides`), and staff spend their wages at **local shops and services**, multiplying the original spend's economic impact (`unless leaked abroad on imports`).
```
