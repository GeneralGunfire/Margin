> **What you'll learn:** why atoms share electrons instead of transferring them in non-metal molecules, how to draw **Lewis dot/cross diagrams** and **Couper (structural) diagrams** accurately, and how **dative covalent bonds** form in $\text{NH}_4^+$ and $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$.
>
> **Prerequisites:** Grade 10 electron configuration, valence electrons (Group number for Groups 1–2 and 13–18), and the octet rule.
>
> **Why this matters:** every VSEPR shape, polarity explanation, and intermolecular force question starts by knowing how many **bonding pairs** and **lone pairs** sit around the central atom in a Lewis structure.

## The idea in plain language

When a metal meets a non-metal (like sodium and chlorine), the non-metal strips electrons away to form ions. When **two non-metals** meet (like hydrogen and oxygen, or two carbon atoms), neither atom wants to give up its electrons — both attract valence electrons strongly.

So they compromise: **they overlap their outer orbitals and SHARE a pair of valence electrons** between the two nuclei!

Why does sharing hold the two atoms together? Because the positively charged nuclei of **both** atoms attract the **negatively charged shared electron cloud** sitting between them, balancing out the repulsion between the two nuclei at an optimal distance!

### Definitions to memorise

```definition
title: Chemical bond
A chemical bond is a mutual attraction between two atoms resulting from the simultaneous attraction between their nuclei and the outer electrons.
```

```definition
title: Covalent bond
A covalent bond is the sharing of at least one pair of electrons between two non-metal atoms.
```

```definition
title: Dative covalent (coordinate) bond
A dative covalent bond is a covalent bond in which **both** of the shared electrons come from **one** of the two bonding atoms (a lone pair donated into an empty orbital, such as $\text{H}^+$).
```

## Lewis vs Couper diagrams

1. **Valence electrons** = the electrons in the outermost energy level (for main-group non-metals: C has 4, N has 5, O has 6, F/Cl/Br have 7, H has 1).
2. **Bonding electron pair:** a shared pair between two atoms (drawn as a shared dot-and-cross pair in Lewis diagrams, or a single line `—` in Couper diagrams).
3. **Lone pair:** a pair of valence electrons on an atom that is **not** shared in bonding. (Exam tip: **ALWAYS draw lone pairs in Lewis diagrams!** Leaving lone pairs off O, N, or Cl loses marks!)
4. **Multiple bonds:**
   - **Single bond:** 1 shared pair (2 electrons), e.g. $\text{H}-\text{Cl}$ or $\text{C}-\text{C}$
   - **Double bond:** 2 shared pairs (4 electrons), e.g. $\text{O}=\text{C}=\text{O}$ or $\text{O}=\text{O}$
   - **Triple bond:** 3 shared pairs (6 electrons), e.g. $\text{N}\equiv\text{N}$ or $\text{H}-\text{C}\equiv\text{N}$

### Step-by-step recipe for any Lewis diagram

```steps
title: Drawing any Lewis structure without guessing
step: Count **total valence electrons** from all atoms in the formula (subtract 1 for each $+$ charge on a cation like $\text{NH}_4^+$; add 1 for each $-$ charge on an anion).
step: Pick the **central atom** (usually the least electronegative atom that is NOT hydrogen — e.g. C, N, S, P, O — because H can only form 1 bond!).
step: Place **one single bond** (1 electron pair) between the central atom and each surrounding atom.
step: Complete **octets** (8 electrons, or 2 for H) on all **outer** atoms first using lone pairs.
step: Place any leftover electrons as **lone pairs on the central atom**.
step: If the central atom still has fewer than 8 electrons (e.g. C in $\text{CO}_2$, N in $\text{N}_2$), slide a lone pair from an outer atom inward to make a **double or triple bond**.
```

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$) — forming double bonds

1. Total valence electrons: $\text{C}(4) + 2 \times \text{O}(6) = 16$ valence electrons ($8$ pairs).
2. Central atom: $\text{C}$ in the middle, connected to two $\text{O}$ atoms (`O — C — O` uses 2 pairs = 4 electrons).
3. Complete octets on outer $\text{O}$ atoms: put 3 lone pairs (6 electrons) on each $\text{O}$. That uses $2 + 3 + 3 = 8$ pairs — all 16 electrons used!
4. Check central $\text{C}$: it only sees **4 electrons** (two single bonds). It needs 4 more!
5. Slide **one lone pair from each oxygen** in to share with carbon $\implies$ **`O=C=O`**, with **2 lone pairs remaining on each O** and **0 lone pairs on C**. Every atom now has 8 valence electrons!

### Example 2: Dative covalent bonding in ammonium ($\text{NH}_4^+$) and hydronium ($\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$)

How does neutral ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) pick up a hydrogen ion ($\text{H}^+$, which has **zero** electrons — just an empty 1s orbital) in water or acid?

- Ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) has **3 N–H covalent bonds** plus **1 lone pair** sitting on nitrogen.
- The $\text{H}^+$ ion needs 2 electrons to achieve a stable helium-like shell, so nitrogen **donates its entire lone pair** into $\text{H}^+$'s empty orbital:

$$\text{NH}_3 + \text{H}^+ \longrightarrow \text{NH}_4^+$$

- Put square brackets around the Lewis diagram of $\text{NH}_4^+$ with a `$+$` outside: nitrogen now shares **4 pairs** with 4 H atoms (often one bond is marked with an arrow $\text{N} \to \text{H}$ to show the dative origin, though once formed all four $\text{N}-\text{H}$ bonds are identical in strength and length!).
- Exactly the same happens when water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$, which has **2 lone pairs** on oxygen) accepts a proton $\text{H}^+$ to form the **hydronium ion ($\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$)** — oxygen keeps **1 lone pair** and has 3 bonding pairs inside brackets `[ ]⁺`.

```tryit
title: Count lone pairs vs bonding pairs
prompt: Draw the Lewis structure of **ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$)** and **water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$)**. How many bonding pairs and how many lone pairs sit around the central atom in each?
hint: N is Group 15 (5 valence electrons); O is Group 16 (6 valence electrons); each H brings 1 electron.
answer: - **$\text{NH}_3$:** 3 bonding pairs (to 3 H atoms) + **1 lone pair** on N.
- **$\text{H}_2\text{O}$:** 2 bonding pairs (to 2 H atoms) + **2 lone pairs** on O.
```

> **Common mistakes**
>
> - Forgetting to draw the **lone pairs** on outer halogen atoms ($\text{F}$, $\text{Cl}$) or central atoms ($\text{N}$ in $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{O}$ in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$). Markers penalise missing lone pairs!
> - Putting more than 2 electrons around **Hydrogen (H)** — hydrogen is in Period 1, so it is full with **2** electrons (1 bond, never double bonds, never lone pairs).
> - Thinking $\text{BeCl}_2$ and $\text{BF}_3$ are mistakes because Be has 4 valence electrons around it and B has 6 — these are valid **incomplete-octet** molecules in CAPS!
> - Forgetting square brackets `[ ... ]⁺` around polyatomic ions like $\text{NH}_4^+$ and $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$.

## Practice

```questions
Q: Draw/describe the Lewis structure of hydrogen cyanide ($\text{HCN}$), stating the number of single bonds, triple bonds, and lone pairs.
A: Total valence electrons $= \text{H}(1) + \text{C}(4) + \text{N}(5) = 10$ electrons ($5$ pairs).
Central atom is $\text{C}$. Single bond $\text{H}-\text{C}$ (2e⁻) and triple bond $\text{C}\equiv\text{N}$ (6e⁻) gives carbon 8 electrons, with **1 lone pair on N** (2e⁻): $\text{H}-\text{C}\equiv\text{N}:$
---
Q: Explain the difference between a normal covalent bond and a dative covalent bond using $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ as an example.
A: In the two original $\text{O}-\text{H}$ bonds of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, oxygen contributes 1 electron and hydrogen contributes 1 electron (normal covalent). When $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ reacts with $\text{H}^+$ (which has 0 electrons), oxygen donates **both** electrons of one of its lone pairs to share with $\text{H}^+$ — forming a **dative covalent bond**.
---
Q: How many shared electron pairs exist between the two nitrogen atoms in $\text{N}_2$, and how many lone pairs remain on each N atom?
A: Each N has 5 valence electrons (10 total). Forming a **triple bond** ($\text{N}\equiv\text{N}$) uses 3 shared pairs (6 electrons), leaving **1 lone pair on each nitrogen atom**.
```
