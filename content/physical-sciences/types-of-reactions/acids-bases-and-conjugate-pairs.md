>

>
> **Why this matters:** Grade 11 Term 4 Acid–Base reactions carry straight over into Grade 12 Paper 2! Once you track where the single $\text{H}^+$ proton moves, conjugate pairs and ampholytes become 100% mechanical.

## The idea in plain language

In Grade 10 you learned Arrhenius's rule (acids make $\text{H}^+$ in water; bases make $\text{OH}^-$). That works in water, **but** ammonia gas ($\text{NH}_3$) neutralises $\text{HCl}$ gas in mid-air with zero water and zero $\text{OH}^-$ ions!

Brønsted and Lowry gave us a universal definition based on **who gives a proton ($\text{H}^+$) away and who catches it**:
- **Acid = Proton ($\text{H}^+$) donor** (gives away ONE $\text{H}^+$).
- **Base = Proton ($\text{H}^+$) acceptor** (catches ONE $\text{H}^+$).

Notice why a hydrogen ion ($\text{H}^+$) is simply called a **"proton"**: a neutral hydrogen atom is 1 proton $+$ 1 electron. Strip away its single electron to make $\text{H}^+$, and literally all that remains is **one naked proton**!

### Definitions to memorise

```definition
title: Brønsted–Lowry acid and base
An **acid** is a proton ($\text{H}^+$) donor.
A **base** is a proton ($\text{H}^+$) acceptor.
```

```definition
title: Ampholyte (amphoteric substance)
An **ampholyte** is a substance that can act as either an **acid** (proton donor) or a **base** (proton acceptor), depending on what it reacts with.
```

```definition
title: Conjugate acid-base pair
A **conjugate acid-base pair** consists of two species on opposite sides of a reaction arrow whose formulas differ by **one single proton ($\text{H}^+$)** only.
```

## How to spot conjugate pairs in 10 seconds

Take any proton-transfer equation, such as ammonia dissolving in water:
$$\text{NH}_3(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\ell) \rightleftharpoons \text{NH}_4^+(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq)$$

Follow the $\text{H}^+$:
1. **$\text{NH}_3$ gains $\text{H}^+$** to become $\text{NH}_4^+$ $\implies$ $\text{NH}_3$ is the **base**, and $\text{NH}_4^+$ is its **conjugate acid**.
2. **$\text{H}_2\text{O}$ loses $\text{H}^+$** to become $\text{OH}^-$ $\implies$ $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is the **acid**, and $\text{OH}^-$ is its **conjugate base**.

Notice the **rule of one $\text{H}^+$**:
- To find the **conjugate acid** of ANY base $\implies$ **add ONE $\text{H}^+$** (formula gains one H, charge goes $+1$!).
- To find the **conjugate base** of ANY acid $\implies$ **remove ONE $\text{H}^+$** (formula loses one H, charge goes $-1$!).

### The classic Grade 11 ampholytes ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{HCO}_3^-$, $\text{HSO}_4^-$, $\text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^-$)

To be an ampholyte, a species must have **at least one $\text{H}$ to donate** (to act as an acid) AND **room/lone pair to accept an $\text{H}^+$** (to act as a base):
- **With a strong acid ($\text{HCl}$):** water accepts $\text{H}^+$ (**base**) $\to \text{H}_3\text{O}^+$.
- **With a base ($\text{NH}_3$):** water donates $\text{H}^+$ (**acid**) $\to \text{OH}^-$.
- **Hydrogen carbonate ion ($\text{HCO}_3^-$):**
  - Gains $\text{H}^+$ (base) $\longrightarrow \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$
  - Loses $\text{H}^+$ (acid) $\longrightarrow \text{CO}_3^{2-}$

## Worked examples

### Example 1 (identifying both conjugate pairs in an exam equation)

Identify the acid, base, and both conjugate acid-base pairs in the reaction:
$$\text{HSO}_4^-(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\ell) \rightleftharpoons \text{SO}_4^{2-}(aq) + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+(aq)$$

```worked
title: Example 1 (Conjugate pairs)
Given: $\text{HSO}_4^-(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\ell) \rightleftharpoons \text{SO}_4^{2-}(aq) + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+(aq)$
Unknown: Acid, base, conjugate acid, conjugate base
Substitution: $\text{HSO}_4^-$ **loses** $\text{H}^+$ to form $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ $\implies$ $\text{HSO}_4^-$ is the **acid**; $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ is its **conjugate base**.
Substitution: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ **gains** $\text{H}^+$ to form $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ $\implies$ $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is the **base**; $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ is its **conjugate acid**.
Answer: Pair 1: $\text{HSO}_4^-$ (acid) / $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ (conjugate base). Pair 2: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (base) / $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ (conjugate acid).
```

### Example 2 (finding conjugate acid vs conjugate base of $\text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^-$)

Write the formula of (a) the **conjugate acid** and (b) the **conjugate base** of dihydrogen phosphate, $\text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^-$.
- (a) **Conjugate acid** = add one $\text{H}^+$: $\text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^- + \text{H}^+ \to \mathbf{\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4}$.
- (b) **Conjugate base** = remove one $\text{H}^+$: $\text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^- - \text{H}^+ \to \mathbf{\text{HPO}_4^{2-}}$.

Notice charge conservation! Adding $+1$ turns $-1$ into $0$; subtracting $+1$ turns $-1$ into $-2$!

```tryit
title: Spot the two-proton trap!
prompt: A learner writes: *"In $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \to 2\text{H}^+ + \text{SO}_4^{2-}$, the conjugate base of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ is $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$."* Why is that incorrect?
hint: How many protons separate a conjugate acid from its conjugate base?
answer: **Only ONE proton ($\text{H}^+$) may differ!** Removing ONE $\text{H}^+$ from $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ gives **$\text{HSO}_4^-$** (the true conjugate base of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$). $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ is the conjugate base of $\text{HSO}_4^-$, differing from $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ by *two* protons!
```

> **Common mistakes**
>
> - Saying $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ and $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ are a conjugate pair (they differ by **two** $\text{H}^+$, not one!).
> - Forgetting to adjust the electrical **charge** by $+1$ or $-1$ when adding/removing $\text{H}^+$.
> - Putting "acid" and "conjugate acid" on the **same** side of the arrow: remember, the **acid + base** sit on the left (reactants), and the **conjugate base + conjugate acid** sit on the right (products)!

## Practice

```questions
Q: Write the conjugate base for each acid: (a) $\text{HNO}_3$, (b) $\text{NH}_4^+$, (c) $\text{HCO}_3^-$, (d) $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$.
A: Remove **one $\text{H}^+$** from each:
(a) $\text{NO}_3^-$, (b) $\text{NH}_3$, (c) $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$, (d) $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
---
Q: Write the conjugate acid for each base: (a) $\text{OH}^-$, (b) $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$, (c) $\text{NH}_3$, (d) $\text{Cl}^-$.
A: Add **one $\text{H}^+$** to each:
(a) $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, (b) $\text{HCO}_3^-$, (c) $\text{NH}_4^+$, (d) $\text{HCl}$.
---
Q: Write two balanced equations showing how the hydrogen sulfite ion ($\text{HSO}_3^-$) acts as an **ampholyte** in water: once reacting as an acid with $\text{OH}^-$, and once reacting as a base with $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$.
A: As an acid (donates $\text{H}^+$): $\text{HSO}_3^-(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \longrightarrow \text{SO}_3^{2-}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\ell)$.
As a base (accepts $\text{H}^+$): $\text{HSO}_3^-(aq) + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+(aq) \longrightarrow \text{H}_2\text{SO}_3(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\ell)$.
```
