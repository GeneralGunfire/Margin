> **What you'll learn:** the four classic acid neutralisation equations (with metal oxides, metal hydroxides, metal carbonates, and ammonia), how to choose the right **acid-base indicator**, and how to solve **volumetric titration** problems accurately without dropping the $1:2$ or $2:1$ mole ratio!
>
> **Prerequisites:** Brønsted–Lowry acids/bases, molarity ($c = n/V$), and stoichiometry.
>
> **Why this matters:** titration calculations combine balanced equations with solution concentration — a guaranteed Paper 2 question in both Grade 11 and Grade 12.

## The idea in plain language

When an acid reacts with a base, their opposing properties cancel out in a **neutralisation reaction**, producing a **salt** (and usually water). The exact products depend on which base you use:

1. **Acid + Metal hydroxide $\longrightarrow$ Salt + Water**
   - $\text{HCl}(aq) + \text{NaOH}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{NaCl}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\ell)$
   - $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4(aq) + 2\,\text{KOH}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{K}_2\text{SO}_4(aq) + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}(\ell)$
2. **Acid + Metal oxide $\longrightarrow$ Salt + Water**
   - $2\,\text{HNO}_3(aq) + \text{CuO}(s) \longrightarrow \text{Cu(NO}_3)_2(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\ell)$
3. **Acid + Metal carbonate (or hydrogencarbonate) $\longrightarrow$ Salt + Water + Carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$ gas fizzing!)**
   - $2\,\text{HCl}(aq) + \text{CaCO}_3(s) \longrightarrow \text{CaCl}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\ell) + \text{CO}_2(g)$
4. **Acid + Ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) $\longrightarrow$ Ammonium salt ONLY (no water written unless $\text{NH}_3$ is aqueous!)**
   - $\text{HCl}(aq) + \text{NH}_3(aq) \longrightarrow \text{NH}_4\text{Cl}(aq)$

*(Bonus reminder from Grade 10: **Acid + Reactive Metal $\longrightarrow$ Salt + Hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$)** — e.g. $\text{Zn} + 2\text{HCl} \to \text{ZnCl}_2 + \text{H}_2$ — which is actually a **redox** reaction, not proton transfer!)*

### Definition to memorise

```definition
title: Standard solution and Equivalence point
A **standard solution** is a solution of **accurately known concentration**.
The **equivalence point** of a titration is the point at which the acid and base have reacted completely in the exact stoichiometric mole ratio shown by the balanced equation. The **endpoint** is the point where the indicator changes colour.
```

## Choosing an acid-base indicator (Grade 11 essentials)

In a **titration**, you slowly run a standard solution from a burette into a measured pipette volume in a conical flask until the indicator changes colour at the **endpoint**. Match the indicator's transition pH to the acid–base strengths:

| Acid + Base pair | Equivalence pH | Best indicator | Colour change (acid $\to$ base) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Strong acid + Strong base** (e.g. $\text{HCl} + \text{NaOH}$) | $\approx 7$ | **Bromothymol blue** | Yellow $\to$ blue (green at pH 7) |
| **Strong acid + Weak base** (e.g. $\text{HCl} + \text{NH}_3$ / $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$) | $< 7$ (acidic) | **Methyl orange** | Red $\to$ orange/yellow ($\text{pH } 3{,}1\text{–}4{,}4$) |
| **Weak acid + Strong base** (e.g. $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{NaOH}$) | $> 7$ (basic) | **Phenolphthalein** | Colourless $\to$ pink ($\text{pH } 8{,}3\text{–}10{,}0$) |

## Titration formula

```formula
title: Volumetric titration stoichiometry
formula: \frac{c_a V_a}{c_b V_b} = \frac{n_a}{n_b}
c_a,\ c_b | molar concentrations of the acid and the base | mol·dm⁻³ | scalar
V_a,\ V_b | volumes of acid and base used to reach the equivalence point (both in **cm³** OR both in **dm³**!) | cm³ or dm³ | scalar
n_a,\ n_b | stoichiometric coefficients from the **balanced chemical equation** | dimensionless | scalar ratio
use: ALWAYS balance the equation first to get n_a and n_b correctly! In monoprotic vs diprotic reactions (like H₂SO₄ + 2 NaOH), n_a = 1 and n_b = 2!
```

## Worked examples

### Example 1 (diprotic acid titration): $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ neutralising $\text{NaOH}$

In a titration, $25{,}0\ \text{cm}^3$ of a $0{,}20\ \text{mol·dm}^{-3}$ sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) solution is completely neutralised by $20{,}0\ \text{cm}^3$ of sulfuric acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$). Calculate the concentration of the sulfuric acid.

First write the balanced equation carefully! Because $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ can donate **two** protons, it takes **two** $\text{NaOH}$ to neutralise **one** $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$:
$$\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4(aq) + 2\,\text{NaOH}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4(aq) + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}(\ell)$$
So $n_a = 1$ and $n_b = 2$!

```worked
title: Example 1 (Titration with 1 : 2 ratio)
Given: $V_b = 25{,}0\ \text{cm}^3$, $c_b = 0{,}20\ \text{mol·dm}^{-3}$, $V_a = 20{,}0\ \text{cm}^3$, $n_a = 1$, $n_b = 2$
Unknown: $c_a$ ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$)
Equation: $\dfrac{c_a V_a}{c_b V_b} = \dfrac{n_a}{n_b}$
Substitution: $\dfrac{c_a (20{,}0)}{(0{,}20)(25{,}0)} = \dfrac{1}{2}$
Substitution: $20{,}0\, c_a = \dfrac{5{,}00}{2} = 2{,}50 \implies c_a = \dfrac{2{,}50}{20{,}0} = 0{,}125\ \text{mol·dm}^{-3}$
Answer: $c(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4) = 0{,}125\ \text{mol·dm}^{-3}$ (or $0{,}13\ \text{mol·dm}^{-3}$ to 2 d.p.).
```

*(Note: You can equally solve via moles directly — $n(\text{NaOH}) = 0{,}20 \times 0{,}0250 = 0{,}0050\ \text{mol} \implies n(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4) = 0{,}0025\ \text{mol} \implies c_a = 0{,}0025 / 0{,}0200 = 0{,}125\ \text{mol·dm}^{-3}$. Both methods are 100% DBE accepted!)*

### Example 2 (naming the salt produced): matching cation + anion

What salt is formed when **nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$)** neutralises **calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$)**?
- Metal cation from the base: **$\text{Ca}^{2+}$**
- Anion from the acid ($\text{HNO}_3 \to \text{H}^+ + \text{NO}_3^-$): **$\text{NO}_3^-$**
- Cross-balance charges ($+2$ and $-1$) $\implies$ **calcium nitrate, $\text{Ca(NO}_3)_2$**!

| Acid | Anion left in salt | Salt family name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$) | $\text{Cl}^-$ | **Chloride** |
| Sulfuric acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) | $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ | **Sulfate** |
| Nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$) | $\text{NO}_3^-$ | **Nitrate** |
| Ethanoic acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) | $\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$ | **Ethanoate** |

```tryit
title: Spot the burette dilution/rinsing mistake!
prompt: Before starting a titration, a learner rinses the **conical flask** with distilled water and leaves drops of pure water inside before pipetting $25{,}0\ \text{cm}^3$ of $\text{NaOH}$ into it. Does leaving distilled water in the conical flask change the titration volume of $\text{HCl}$ needed?
hint: Does adding pure water change the **number of moles ($n$)** of $\text{NaOH}$ already measured by the pipette?
answer: **No — it makes zero difference!** Adding pure water lowers concentration temporarily, **but** the **moles ($n$) of $\text{NaOH}$ inside the flask remain exact**. (By contrast, rinsing the *burette* or *pipette* with water instead of the solution it will hold *does* dilute the delivered moles and ruins the result!)
```

> **Common mistakes**
>
> - Using $c_1 V_1 = c_2 V_2$ blindly for titrations! That ONLY works when the acid–base mole ratio is $1 : 1$ — it fails completely for $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\,\text{NaOH}$ or $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + 2\,\text{HCl}$! Always include $\dfrac{n_a}{n_b}$.
> - Mixing $\text{cm}^3$ on top and $\text{dm}^3$ on bottom in $\dfrac{c_a V_a}{c_b V_b}$. Keep $V_a$ and $V_b$ in the **same unit** (or convert both to $\text{dm}^3$ if finding moles $n = cV$).
> - Confusing **strong/weak acid** (degree of ionisation in water: e.g. $\text{HCl}$ ionises $\approx 100\%$, $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ ionises partially) with **concentrated/dilute** (number of moles dissolved per $\text{dm}^3$). You can have a *dilute strong acid* ($0{,}01\ \text{mol·dm}^{-3}\ \text{HCl}$) and a *concentrated weak acid* ($5\ \text{mol·dm}^{-3}$ ethanoic acid)!

## Practice

```questions
Q: Write a balanced chemical equation for the reaction between sulfuric acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) and sodium carbonate ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$).
A: $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4(aq) + \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3(s) \longrightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\ell) + \text{CO}_2(g)$.
---
Q: $30{,}0\ \text{cm}^3$ of oxalic acid ($(\text{COOH})_2$, diprotic: $1\ (\text{COOH})_2 + 2\,\text{NaOH} \to \text{Na}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is neutralised by $24{,}0\ \text{cm}^3$ of $0{,}25\ \text{mol·dm}^{-3}\ \text{NaOH}$. (a) Calculate the concentration of the oxalic acid. (b) Which indicator should be used?
A: (a) $\dfrac{c_a(30{,}0)}{(0{,}25)(24{,}0)} = \dfrac{1}{2} \implies 30{,}0\,c_a = 3{,}00 \implies c_a = 0{,}10\ \text{mol·dm}^{-3}$.
(b) Oxalic acid is a **weak acid** and $\text{NaOH}$ is a **strong base**, so the equivalence point is $> 7$: choose **phenolphthalein**.
---
Q: What volume of $0{,}50\ \text{mol·dm}^{-3}\ \text{HCl}$ is needed to react completely with $1{,}06\ \text{g}$ of pure $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ ($M = 106\ \text{g·mol}^{-1}$)?
A: $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + 2\,\text{HCl} \to 2\,\text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$.
$n(\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3) = \dfrac{1{,}06}{106} = 0{,}010\ \text{mol}$.
$n(\text{HCl}) = 2 \times 0{,}010 = 0{,}020\ \text{mol}$.
$V(\text{HCl}) = \dfrac{n}{c} = \dfrac{0{,}020}{0{,}50} = 0{,}040\ \text{dm}^3 = 40{,}0\ \text{cm}^3$.
```
