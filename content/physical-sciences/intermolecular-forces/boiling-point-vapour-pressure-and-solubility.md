> **What you'll learn:** how intermolecular force strength controls five observable macroscopic properties — **boiling point**, **melting point**, **vapour pressure**, **viscosity**, and **solubility ("like dissolves like")** — and the exact 3-step template DBE Paper 2 markers want in every explanation question.
>
> **Prerequisites:** identifying IMFs (London dispersion, dipole–dipole, hydrogen bonding, ion–dipole) and polar vs non-polar molecules.
>
> **Why this matters:** Paper 2 invariably gives you a data table of 3–4 liquids with their boiling points or vapour pressures and asks for 3–4 mark explanations comparing them.

## The idea in plain language

Imagine molecules in a liquid holding hands with their neighbours:
- **Strong IMFs = tight grip.** Molecules resist escaping into the air, resist sliding past one another, and refuse to let go until heated to a high temperature!
- **Weak IMFs = loose grip.** Molecules evaporate easily even at room temperature and boil at low temperatures.

Here is the complete physical consequences map of **stronger intermolecular forces**:

| Physical property | If IMFs are STRONGER... | Why (microscopic picture) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Boiling point ($T_b$)** | **HIGHER** | More thermal energy is needed for liquid molecules to break free into gas against $P_{\text{atm}}$. |
| **Melting point ($T_m$)** | **HIGHER** | More energy is needed to loosen the rigid solid lattice. |
| **Vapour pressure ($P_{\text{vap}}$)** | **LOWER** | Fewer molecules have enough energy to escape the liquid surface into vapour at that temperature! |
| **Evaporation rate** | **SLOWER** | Harder for surface molecules to break away. |
| **Viscosity** (resistance to flow) | **HIGHER** (thicker, e.g. syrup/glycerol) | Strong IMFs (plus long tangled shapes) stop molecules sliding smoothly past each other. |
| **Surface tension** | **HIGHER** | Surface molecules are pulled tightly inward by neighbours below. |

> **Remember the inverse relationship:** **Higher boiling point $\Longleftrightarrow$ Lower vapour pressure** (at the same temperature)! A volatile liquid like acetone or petrol evaporates rapidly (high vapour pressure) because its IMFs are weak, so it boils easily (low boiling point).

### Definitions to memorise

```definition
title: Boiling point
The temperature at which the vapour pressure of a substance equals atmospheric (external) pressure.
```

```definition
title: Vapour pressure
The pressure exerted by a vapour at equilibrium with its liquid (or solid) in a closed container at a specific temperature.
```

## The 3-mark DBE Paper 2 answer template

Whenever an exam asks: *"Explain the difference in boiling point (or vapour pressure) between Substance A and Substance B"*, hit these **three exact bullet points** every time:

```steps
title: Full-mark IMF explanation checklist
step: **Name the specific IMF** present between molecules of Substance A and between molecules of Substance B (e.g. hydrogen bonds vs dipole–dipole vs London dispersion forces).
step: **Compare the relative strength** of those IMFs (and state why — e.g. hydrogen bonding is stronger than dipole–dipole, OR larger molar mass / chain surface area gives stronger London forces).
step: **Link explicitly to energy needed:** *"Therefore **more energy is required to overcome** the intermolecular forces in [stronger liquid], resulting in a **higher boiling point / lower vapour pressure**."*
```

## Solubility: "Like dissolves like"

Why does salt ($\text{NaCl}$) dissolve effortlessly in water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$), while cooking oil (non-polar) floats separately on top of water?

A solute dissolves only when the **new forces formed between solute and solvent** are strong enough to replace the forces holding pure solute together and pure solvent together:
- **Polar / Ionic solvents (like $\text{H}_2\text{O}$)** dissolve **polar molecules** (like $\text{NH}_3$, ethanol, sugar — forming dipole–dipole or hydrogen bonds with water) and **ionic solids** (like $\text{NaCl}$ — forming strong **ion–dipole** attractions).
- **Non-polar solvents (like hexane, $\text{CCl}_4$, petrol)** dissolve **non-polar solutes** (like iodine $\text{I}_2$, candle wax, grease — because both have weak **London dispersion forces** of comparable strength!).
- Mixing **non-polar oil + polar water** fails because water molecules strongly prefer hydrogen-bonding to each other and squeeze the non-polar oil molecules out!

## Worked examples

### Example 1 (interpreting a vapour pressure vs temperature graph/table)

At $20^\circ\text{C}$, the vapour pressure of ethanol ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$, $M = 46\ \text{g·mol}^{-1}$) is $5{,}9\ \text{kPa}$, while the vapour pressure of propanone/acetone ($\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3$, $M = 58\ \text{g·mol}^{-1}$) is $24{,}7\ \text{kPa}$.
(a) Which substance has the higher boiling point? Explain.
(b) Explain the difference in vapour pressure in terms of intermolecular forces.

```worked
title: Example 1 (Vapour pressure -> IMFs -> Boiling point)
Given: At $20^\circ\text{C}$, $P_{\text{vap}}(\text{ethanol}) = 5{,}9\ \text{kPa}$ and $P_{\text{vap}}(\text{acetone}) = 24{,}7\ \text{kPa}$
Unknown: Higher boiling point and IMF explanation
Answer: (a) **Ethanol** has the higher boiling point. Because ethanol has a **lower vapour pressure** at $20^\circ\text{C}$, it requires heating to a higher temperature before its vapour pressure reaches atmospheric pressure ($101{,}3\ \text{kPa}$).
Answer: (b) Ethanol molecules contain an $-\text{O}-\text{H}$ group and form strong **hydrogen bonds** (plus London forces) between molecules. Acetone molecules are polar with no $\text{O}-\text{H}$ bond, so they have weaker **dipole–dipole forces** (plus London forces). The **stronger** hydrogen bonds in ethanol require **more energy to overcome**, so fewer molecules escape into the vapour phase at $20^\circ\text{C}$ — giving ethanol a **lower vapour pressure**.
```

### Example 2 (viscosity & surface tension): glycerol vs water

Why is glycerol ($\text{HOCH}_2\text{CH(OH)CH}_2\text{OH}$) far thicker (**more viscous**) than water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$)?
- Water has **one** oxygen with two $-\text{OH}$ bonds; glycerol has **three $-\text{OH}$ groups** per molecule!
- That allows each glycerol molecule to form **many extensive hydrogen bonds** (plus larger molar mass = stronger London forces) with neighbouring molecules, tangling them together so they resist flowing past each other.

```tryit
title: Predict which liquid is used to clean iodine stains!
prompt: Solid iodine ($\text{I}_2$) barely dissolves in pure water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$), turning it pale yellow, yet dissolves rapidly in carbon tetrachloride ($\text{CCl}_4$) to give a deep purple solution. Explain why using intermolecular forces.
hint: Classify the polarity of $\text{I}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, and $\text{CCl}_4$.
answer: **$\text{I}_2$ and $\text{CCl}_4$ are both NON-POLAR** (only London dispersion forces), so $\text{I}_2$ molecules easily mix with $\text{CCl}_4$ forming comparable London dispersion attractions ("like dissolves like"). Water molecules hold onto each other via strong **hydrogen bonds**, which are much stronger than weak dipole–induced dipole forces with $\text{I}_2$, so water excludes most $\text{I}_2$.
```

> **Common mistakes**
>
> - Saying *"stronger intermolecular forces give HIGHER vapour pressure"*. Think backwards: tight grip = **harder to evaporate** = **FEWER** gas molecules escaping = **LOWER vapour pressure**!
> - Saying *"covalent bonds in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ require more energy to break when boiling"*. Say **"energy required to overcome intermolecular forces"** — NEVER "break covalent bonds"!
> - Forgetting to name BOTH substances' IMFs when asked to *compare* two liquids.

## Practice

```questions
Q: At $1\ \text{atm}$ ($101{,}3\ \text{kPa}$), propane ($\text{C}_3\text{H}_8$) boils at $-42^\circ\text{C}$ while water boils at $100^\circ\text{C}$, even though their molar masses are $44$ and $18\ \text{g·mol}^{-1}$. Account for this large difference.
A: Propane is non-polar, so only weak **London dispersion forces** act between $\text{C}_3\text{H}_8$ molecules. Water has strong **hydrogen bonds** between $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules. Because hydrogen bonds are much stronger than London forces, far **more energy is needed to overcome** the intermolecular forces in water, giving water a much higher boiling point.
---
Q: Liquid $X$ boils at $56^\circ\text{C}$ and liquid $Y$ boils at $118^\circ\text{C}$. At $25^\circ\text{C}$, which liquid has (a) the higher vapour pressure and (b) the weaker intermolecular forces?
A: (a) **Liquid $X$** has the higher vapour pressure at $25^\circ\text{C}$ (it evaporates more easily and needs less heating to reach $101{,}3\ \text{kPa}$).
(b) **Liquid $X$** has the weaker intermolecular forces.
---
Q: Why does pressure-cooking boil food faster, while cooking pasta at the top of a high mountain (low atmospheric pressure) takes longer in open water?
A: Water boils when its **vapour pressure equals external atmospheric pressure**. On a high mountain, atmospheric pressure is lower, so water reaches boiling at a **lower temperature** (say $88^\circ\text{C}$ instead of $100^\circ\text{C}$), cooking food more slowly. Inside a sealed pressure cooker, external pressure is high, so water only boils above $100^\circ\text{C}$ ($\approx 120^\circ\text{C}$), cooking food much faster!
```
