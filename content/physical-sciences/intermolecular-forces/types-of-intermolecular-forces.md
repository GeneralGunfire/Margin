> **What you'll learn:** the vital difference between **intramolecular (interatomic) chemical bonds** and **intermolecular forces (IMFs)**, how to identify the exact IMF between any pair of molecules or ions, and why hydrogen bonding makes water behave unlike almost any other small molecule.
>
> **Prerequisites:** polar vs non-polar molecules and electronegativity ($\Delta\text{EN}$).
>
> **Why this matters:** Intermolecular Forces is tested heavily in BOTH Grade 11 Paper 2 and Grade 12 Paper 2 (Organic Chemistry physical properties). Every boiling-point question asks you to name the **type of IMF**, compare **strength**, and link to **energy needed to overcome**.

## The idea in plain language

Picture a pot of liquid water boiling on a stove into steam:
- Does the water split into hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$) and oxygen gas ($\text{O}_2$)? **NO!** Steam is still $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules!
- That proves the strong **covalent $\text{O}-\text{H}$ bonds INSIDE** each water molecule do **not** break when water boils! What breaks are the much weaker **attractions BETWEEN separate water molecules**.

This distinction is worth easy marks in Paper 2:
- **Intramolecular (interatomic) bonds:** strong forces **inside** a molecule holding atoms together (covalent, ionic, metallic). These break only during **chemical reactions**.
- **Intermolecular forces (IMFs):** weaker electrostatic attractions **between** neighbouring molecules (or between ions and molecules). These are overcome during **phase changes** (melting, boiling, evaporation).

### Definition to memorise

```definition
title: Intermolecular forces
Intermolecular forces are forces of attraction between molecules (or between atoms of noble gases), which are much weaker than intramolecular chemical bonds.
```

## The five types of intermolecular forces (CAPS classification)

Under Van der Waals forces and ion interactions, CAPS classifies five IMFs from strongest to weakest (for molecules of similar size):

1. **Ion–dipole forces:** attraction between an ion ($\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Cl}^-$) and the opposite partial charge ($\delta^-$, $\delta^+$) of a polar molecule (like $\text{H}_2\text{O}$). *Example:* $\text{NaCl}$ dissolving in water!
2. **Ion–induced dipole forces:** attraction when an ion approaches a non-polar molecule and temporarily distorts its electron cloud. *Example:* $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ ions in haemoglobin binding non-polar $\text{O}_2$.
3. **Dipole–dipole forces (Van der Waals):** attraction between the $\delta^+$ end of one **polar** molecule and the $\delta^-$ end of a neighbouring **polar** molecule. *Examples:* $\text{HCl}$, $\text{SO}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{S}$.
   - **Special strong case — Hydrogen bonding:** NOT a real covalent bond! It is an extra-strong **dipole–dipole attraction** occurring ONLY when **H is covalently bonded directly to a small, highly electronegative atom: N, O, or F** ($\text{H}-\text{N}$, $\text{H}-\text{O}$, or $\text{H}-\text{F}$), attracting a lone pair on a neighbouring N, O, or F! *Examples:* $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{HF}$, alcohols.
4. **Dipole–induced dipole forces:** a permanent polar molecule (like $\text{H}_2\text{O}$) pushes electrons in a nearby non-polar molecule (like $\text{O}_2$), creating a weak temporary dipole. *Example:* oxygen gas dissolving slightly in river water so fish can breathe!
5. **London dispersion / induced dipole forces (Van der Waals):** **present between ALL molecules**, and the **ONLY** IMF between **non-polar molecules** and noble-gas atoms! Even in symmetrical non-polar $\text{I}_2$ or $\text{CH}_4$, electrons slosh randomly for a split second, creating a fleeting instantaneous dipole that induces a matching dipole in its neighbour.
   - **Rule for strength:** Larger molecules have **more electrons** (larger molar mass and larger electron cloud / contact surface area), making London forces **stronger**! That is why at room temperature $\text{F}_2$ and $\text{Cl}_2$ are gases, $\text{Br}_2$ is a liquid, and $\text{I}_2$ is a solid!

### Quick decision flowchart: which IMF dominates between two identical molecules?

```steps
title: Identifying the dominant IMF in 3 questions
step: **Is the substance ionic in water (e.g. $\text{NaCl}(aq)$)?** $\to$ **Ion–dipole forces**.
step: **Is the molecule POLAR (asymmetrical)?**
- If **NO (non-polar / symmetrical**, like $\text{CH}_4$, $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{CCl}_4$, $\text{Br}_2$, $\text{He}$): **London dispersion (induced dipole) forces ONLY!**
- If **YES (polar)**: go to step 3!
step: **Does the polar molecule contain H bonded directly to N, O, or F?**
- If **YES** ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{HF}$): **Hydrogen bonding** (strongest Van der Waals IMF, plus underlying London forces).
- If **NO** ($\text{HCl}$, $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, $\text{SO}_2$, $\text{CHCl}_3$): **Dipole–dipole forces** (plus underlying London forces).
```

## Worked examples

### Example 1 (halogens trend — London forces vs molar mass)

At $25^\circ\text{C}$, $\text{Cl}_2$ is a gas ($T_b = -34^\circ\text{C}$), $\text{Br}_2$ is a liquid ($T_b = 59^\circ\text{C}$), and $\text{I}_2$ is a solid ($T_b = 184^\circ\text{C}$). Explain this trend in boiling points.

- All three ($\text{Cl}_2$, $\text{Br}_2$, $\text{I}_2$) are **non-polar** diatomic molecules, so the only intermolecular forces between their molecules are **London dispersion (induced dipole) forces**.
- From $\text{Cl}_2$ to $\text{Br}_2$ to $\text{I}_2$, **molecular mass and electron-cloud size increase**, so the London dispersion forces become **stronger**.
- **More energy is required** to overcome the stronger intermolecular forces between $\text{I}_2$ molecules, so boiling point increases from $\text{Cl}_2$ to $\text{I}_2$.

### Example 2 (Group 16 hydrides anomaly): why is $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ liquid while $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ is a foul gas?

Check the explorer diagram above! $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ is heavier ($M = 34\ \text{g·mol}^{-1}$) than $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ ($M = 18\ \text{g·mol}^{-1}$), yet $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ boils at $-60^\circ\text{C}$ while $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ boils at $+100^\circ\text{C}$!
- **$\text{H}_2\text{S}$** has weaker **dipole–dipole forces** (plus London forces) because sulfur is not electronegative or small enough for hydrogen bonding.
- **$\text{H}_2\text{O}$** has **hydrogen bonding** (each $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ forms up to four hydrogen bonds because O has two H atoms and two lone pairs!), which are **much stronger** than dipole–dipole forces in $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ and require **far more energy** to overcome.

```tryit
title: Spot the hydrogen-bonding impostor!
prompt: Both **$\text{CH}_3\text{F}$** (fluoromethane) and **$\text{HF}$** contain hydrogen and fluorine. Which one forms **hydrogen bonds** between its own molecules, and why?
hint: Is the hydrogen in $\text{CH}_3\text{F}$ attached to carbon or directly to fluorine?
answer: **Only $\text{HF}$!** In $\text{CH}_3\text{F}$, all three H atoms are bonded to **carbon** (`H-C-F`), NOT to fluorine! So $\text{CH}_3\text{F}$ only has ordinary **dipole–dipole** forces (plus London forces), whereas $\text{HF}$ has an $\text{H}-\text{F}$ bond and forms **hydrogen bonds**.
```

> **Common mistakes**
>
> - Calling a hydrogen bond an *intramolecular* covalent bond. Hydrogen bonding is an **intermolecular** force *between* molecules (roughly $10\times$ weaker than a covalent $\text{O}-\text{H}$ bond inside a molecule!).
> - Claiming that polar molecules like $\text{HCl}$ do *not* have London forces. **ALL** molecules have London dispersion forces; polar molecules have dipole–dipole *in addition* to London forces.
> - Writing "covalent bonds break when $\text{CO}_2$ sublimes or water boils". Phase changes break **intermolecular forces**, never covalent bonds!

## Practice

```questions
Q: Identify the strongest type of intermolecular force present in pure samples of each substance: (a) $\text{NH}_3(\ell)$, (b) $\text{CCl}_4(\ell)$, (c) $\text{HBr}(g)$, (d) $\text{KBr}(aq)$.
A: (a) **Hydrogen bonding** (H bonded directly to N).
(b) **London dispersion (induced dipole) forces** ($\text{CCl}_4$ is symmetrical and non-polar).
(c) **Dipole–dipole forces** ($\text{HBr}$ is polar, and Br is too large/less electronegative for H-bonding).
(d) **Ion–dipole forces** (between $\text{K}^+$/$\text{Br}^-$ ions and polar $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules).
---
Q: Both $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ (ethane, $M = 30\ \text{g·mol}^{-1}$) and $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{14}$ (hexane, $M = 86\ \text{g·mol}^{-1}$) are non-polar molecules. State which one has the higher boiling point and explain fully.
A: **Hexane ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{14}$)** has the higher boiling point. Both are non-polar with **London dispersion forces** only, but hexane has a larger molecular mass and surface area (more electrons), producing **stronger London dispersion forces** that require **more energy to overcome**.
---
Q: Why does **ice float** on liquid water, unlike almost every other substance whose solid sinks in its own liquid?
A: In liquid water, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules pack fairly closely. When water freezes into ice, **hydrogen bonds** lock the molecules into an open hexagonal crystal lattice with empty spaces between them — making ice **less dense** than liquid water!
```
