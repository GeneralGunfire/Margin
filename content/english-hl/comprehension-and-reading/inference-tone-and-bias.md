> **What you'll learn:** how to separate **tone** (writer's attitude) from **mood** (reader's feeling) and **style/register**, how to identify a writer's **intention**, how to distinguish **fact from opinion**, and how to spot **bias, prejudice, and loaded language**.
>
> **Prerequisites:** literal vs inferential reading.
>
> **Why this matters:** *"Comment on the writer's tone"*, *"What is the writer's intention?"*, and *"Explain why paragraph 4 is biased"* appear in almost every single Grade 11 and Grade 12 Paper 1 exam — and vague answers like *"the tone is negative"* or *"the mood is bad"* get zero marks.

## The idea in plain language

Two writers can report the exact same event — a school cell-phone ban — and sound completely different:
- Writer A: *"Research confirms screen-free classrooms lift focus scores by 14%, though parents still value emergency contact."* (**Measured, objective, informative**)
- Writer B: *"At last, schools are reclaiming young minds from the zombie glow of addictive pocket screens."* (**Assertive, sarcastic, urgent**)

Paper 1 tests whether you can hear the **voice** behind the words — not just *what* is said, but **how** the writer feels about it (**tone**), **why** they wrote it (**intention**), and whether they are slanting the truth (**bias**).

### Definitions to memorise

```definition
title: Tone vs Mood
**Tone** is the writer's or speaker's **attitude** toward the subject or audience, revealed through diction and punctuation (e.g. *cynical, admiring, indignant, objective*).
**Mood** is the overall **emotional atmosphere** created in the text and felt by the reader (e.g. *tense, melancholic, festive, ominous*).
```

```definition
title: Fact vs Opinion vs Bias
A **fact** is a statement that can be objectively verified or proved true/false.
An **opinion** is a personal belief, judgement, or interpretation that cannot be proved true.
**Bias** is a one-sided, unbalanced presentation that favours or opposes a person, group, or viewpoint unfairly, ignoring valid counter-arguments.
```

## Choosing accurate tone words (ban "good", "bad", "positive", "negative"!)

Never write *"The tone is negative"* or *"The tone is sad"* in Home Language! Always use a precise adjective backed by **one quoted word or phrase** showing *where* you hear that attitude:

| Attitude group | Precise Paper 1 tone words | Clues to look for in the text |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Supportive / admiring** | *reverent, appreciative, enthusiastic, optimistic, empathetic* | Warm praise, uplifting metaphors, hopeful future tense |
| **Critical / angry** | *scathing, indignant, cynical, dismissive, condemning, outraged* | Sharp verbs, exclamation marks, harsh loaded adjectives |
| **Mocking / humorous** | *sarcastic, satirical, ironic, wry, facetious, mocking* | Exaggeration, saying the opposite of what is meant, scare quotes |
| **Neutral / scholarly** | *objective, measured, analytical, matter-of-fact, balanced* | Statistics, third-person prose, hedging (*"suggests"*, *"on the other hand"*) |
| **Warning / serious** | *urgent, cautionary, ominous, sombre, pleading* | Imperatives (*"must"*), dire predictions, rhetorical questions |

## How to spot bias and writer's intention

Every text is written with a **purpose (intention)**: to **inform**, **persuade**, **entertain**, **warn**, **criticise/satirise**, or **reflect**.

When a question asks: *"Is the writer biased? Substantiate your response"*, check for these four red flags of **bias**:
1. **Loaded / emotive diction:** choosing slanted words instead of neutral ones (calling protesters *"an unruly mob"* vs *"demonstrators"*, or calling an idea *"a reckless gamble"* vs *"an experiment"*).
2. **One-sided evidence:** quoting only experts who agree while ignoring or mocking the opposing view.
3. **Unsubstantiated generalisations:** sweeping claims like *"All teenagers refuse to read"* or *"Everyone knows..."*.
4. **Rhetorical questions & imperatives:** forcing the reader to agree (*"How much longer must taxpayers fund this farce?"*).

```compare
title: Objective reporting vs Biased writing
left: Balanced / Objective
- Presents statistics, dates, or named sources accurately.
- Acknowledges both advantages and drawbacks (*"However, critics argue..."*).
- Uses neutral verbs (*stated, reported, estimated*).
right: Biased / Persuasive
- Uses emotive adjectives and exaggeration (*"catastrophic failure"*, *"miracle cure"*).
- Omits counter-arguments completely.
- Uses sweeping words (*"always"*, *"never"*, *"obviously"*).
```

## Worked example

Read this short editorial extract:

> *"While local commuters wait forty minutes in pouring rain, city transport officials congratulate themselves inside heated boardrooms over 'world-class upgrades'. Nobody denies new buses cost money — yet pouring millions into painted bike lanes nobody uses instead of fixing broken engines borders on farce."*

**Questions & model answers:**

1. **Identify the writer's tone in `"city transport officials congratulate themselves inside heated boardrooms over 'world-class upgrades'"` and substantiate your answer. (2 marks)**
   - **Answer:** The tone is **sarcastic / scathing / cynical** (1). By placing `'world-class upgrades'` in quotation marks and contrasting officials' comfortable `"heated boardrooms"` with commuters standing `"in pouring rain"`, the writer mocks their self-praise as out of touch (1).
2. **Identify the writer's intention in this extract. (2 marks)**
   - **Answer:** To **criticise / expose** municipal transport mismanagement (1) and **persuade** readers/authorities to prioritise reliable bus maintenance over cosmetic projects (1).
3. **Explain whether this extract is biased, quoting ONE piece of evidence. (2 marks)**
   - **Answer:** Yes, it is biased/one-sided because it omits any explanation from the officials for the bike-lane budget and uses exaggerated, sweeping phrasing such as `"bike lanes nobody uses"` or `"borders on farce"` to steer the reader against the municipality.

```tryit
title: Spot the tone vs mood confusion!
prompt: A poem describes a lonely soldier walking past burnt fields under grey clouds. A learner writes: *"The poet's tone is dark and foggy."* Fix this answer for full marks!
hint: "Dark and foggy" describes the **setting / atmosphere (mood)** — what is the poet's **attitude** toward war or the soldier?
answer: **Mood** is bleak, desolate, or oppressive (created by "burnt fields" and "grey clouds"). **Tone** is the speaker's attitude — for example, **sombre, mournful, or quietly critical** of the destruction caused by war. Never confuse weather/scenery with the speaker's tone!
```

> **Common mistakes**
>
> - Writing `"positive tone"` or `"negative tone"` — Grade 11 Home Language requires specific tone adjectives (*ironic, compassionate, sceptical, patronising*).
> - Answering an intention question with `"to tell us about..."` — state the active communicative verb (*to persuade, to warn, to critique, to inform objectively, to satirise*) PLUS *what* specifically.
> - Claiming a passage is "not biased" just because it contains one statistic — a clever biased writer cherry-picks real statistics while hiding all counter-evidence!

## Practice

```questions
Q: Identify whether each statement is a **fact**, an **opinion**, or a **biased generalisation**:
(a) South Africa hosts eleven official spoken languages plus South African Sign Language.
(b) Clearly, only careless drivers ever cause traffic delays on the N1.
(c) Jazz remains far more expressive than classical music.
A: (a) **Fact** (verifiable constitutional/factual information).
(b) **Biased generalisation** (sweeping claim using *"only"* and *"ever"*, ignoring roadworks, weather, or mechanical failure).
(c) **Opinion** (personal value judgement — cannot be objectively proved).
---
Q: Read: *"Oh brilliant — another three-hour power cut right before my assignment deadline!"* Name the tone and explain the device used. (2)
A: **Sarcastic / frustrated / exasperated** tone (1). Calling a blackout `"brilliant"` uses **verbal irony (sarcasm)** — saying the exact opposite of what the speaker genuinely feels (1).
---
Q: Why might an author open an opinion piece with a brief, moving personal story before presenting national data? (2)
A: The personal anecdote creates an **emotional connection / empathy** with the reader first (pathos), making the reader more receptive to the statistical argument (logos) that follows.
```
