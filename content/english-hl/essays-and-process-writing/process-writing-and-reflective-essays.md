> **What you'll learn:** how to run the five-stage **process-writing workflow** (brainstorming, planning, drafting, editing, proofreading) under exam conditions, how Paper 3's **50 marks** are weighted (Content & Planning 30, Language, Style & Editing 15, Structure 5), and how to write convincing **narrative**, **descriptive**, and **reflective** essays without clichés or forced "morals".
>
> **Prerequisites:** paragraph structure, tense consistency, and figurative diction.
>
> **Why this matters:** Paper 3 is worth **100 marks** — the single highest-scoring written paper in English HL, and the essay alone carries **50 marks** (half the paper)! Strong planning separates a top-band original essay (`40+ / 50`) from a rushed story that loses focus halfway through.

## The idea in plain language

Most learners lose marks in Paper 3 essays **before they even write sentence one** — they pick a prompt, jump straight onto lined paper, realise halfway through that they have no ending, and rush a melodramatic twist (*"and then I woke up, it was all a dream"* or *"and suddenly a car hit him"*).

Markers immediately spot an unplanned essay. By spending **10–12 minutes planning**, you lock down three things before drafting:
1. **A clear controlling idea or arc** (what actually changes from paragraph 1 to the final paragraph).
2. **A tight focus in time and place** (one meaningful afternoon, one difficult conversation, or one turning-point memory — NOT an entire 15-year life story).
3. **Consistent narrative voice and tense** from beginning to end.

### How the 50 marks are awarded (DBE HL Essay Rubric)

- **Content and Planning (30 marks — 60%):** originality, depth of thought/emotion, staying strictly focused on the prompt (no "pre-prepared memorised story forced onto the topic"), and visible **mind-map/outline planning**.
- **Language, Style and Editing (15 marks — 30%):** varied sentence lengths, vivid sensory vocabulary, apt figurative language, accurate punctuation/spelling, and tone suited to the genre.
- **Structure (5 marks — 10%):** gripping opening hook, smooth paragraph links, purposeful pacing, and a resonant ending.

## The 5-stage process writing method (exam edition)

```steps
title: How to spend your 80 minutes on Section A (400–450 words)
step: **1. Decode & Brainstorm (5 min):** Read ALL prompts (including visual picture prompts). Underline key words or metaphors in your chosen topic. Jot sensory memories, contrasts, and angles on a planning page.
step: **2. Plan & Structure (5–7 min):** Build a quick 5-box plan: Hook (Opening scene/image) -> Setup/Context -> Turning point or deepening reflection -> Climax/Shift in insight -> Echo ending. Write `"PLANNING"` clearly at the top and draw one neat diagonal line through it later if your school prefers, leaving it readable for the marker!
step: **3. Draft with Control (45 min):** Write cleanly, aiming for **400–450 words** (roughly 1.5–2 pages of normal handwriting — never pad endlessly to 700 words, which increases error rate).
step: **4. Revise Structure & Register (10 min):** Check that every paragraph connects back to the prompt, cut repetitive filler (`very`, `really`, `nice`, `thing`), and sharpen verbs.
step: **5. Proofread Mechanics (10 min):** Run a fast three-pass scan specifically for **tense shifts**, **comma splices**, and **homophones** (`their/there`, `its/it's`, `affect/effect`).
```

## Choosing between Narrative, Descriptive, and Reflective essays

### 1. Narrative Essay — *Tell a tight, character-driven story*

- **Golden rule:** Start **close to the action** (*in media res*), keep characters to **1–3 people**, and let **conflict come from a real choice or internal tension**, not sensational soap-opera accidents.
- **Show, do not tell:** Instead of *"I was terrified"*, show physical and sensory reactions: *"My thumb worried the brass zip of my blazer until my knuckles whitened."*
- **Dialogue discipline:** Use dialogue sparingly (max 4–6 exchanges) ONLY when it reveals character or escalates tension — format every new speaker on a **new line**.

### 2. Descriptive Essay — *Paint a living mood through senses*

- **Golden rule:** Great description is NOT a dictionary dump of five adjectives per noun (*"the colossal, shimmering, azure, vast ocean"*). Choose **one precise verb and one fresh image** (*"the bay breathed salt onto the cracked jetty"*).
- **Use all five senses + movement:** Anchor the scene with a clear **perspective** (watching a taxi rank wake up at 05:30; sitting in an empty school hall after exams) and shift mood naturally as light, sound, or activity changes.

### 3. Reflective Essay — *Explore a personal insight honestly*

- **Golden rule:** A reflective essay starts from a **concrete trigger** (an old pair of running shoes, a grandparent's silent kitchen, failing a trial you expected to win) and **unpacks how your understanding changed**.
- **Avoid preaching:** Never lecture the reader with *"Everyone in society should learn that..."*. Keep reflection anchored in authentic, nuanced self-observation.

```compare
- Trusts the reader to feel the insight without spelling out a cliché moral.
```

## Worked example: Decoding a Visual Prompt

**Prompt:** *A photograph shows an open suitcase beside a cracked wooden front door, half in shadow and half in morning sunlight.*

1. **Weak literal trap:** Describing packing socks and catching a bus for 400 words without meaning.
2. **Strong Grade 11 angle (Reflective/Narrative hybrid):** Treat the **threshold between shadow and morning sun** as a quiet metaphor for leaving home after matric/moving townships — focusing on the **one hour before departure**, the unspoken pride and fear between parent and child, and one small object left behind on the table.
3. **Why this scores 40+/50:** Tight timeframe, clear emotional arc, authentic South African sensory detail, and subtle connection to the visual prompt without once saying *"In this picture I see..."*.

```tryit
title: Upgrade "telling" into "showing"
prompt: Rewrite this flat sentence into **2 vivid, sensory sentences** suitable for a Grade 11 descriptive or narrative essay (no purple-prose adjective piles!):
*"The classroom was very hot and everyone was bored while waiting for the exam paper."*
hint: Focus on one specific sound, one physical sensation, and one small visible detail.
answer: **Example upgrade:** *"Heat hung stagnant against the high windows, turning the wall clock's slow click into the loudest sound in the room. Ahead of me, a boy traced the splintered edge of his desk with his fingernail while sweat beaded along the nape of his neck."*
```

> **Common mistakes**
>
> - Writing `"In this essay I will write about..."` or `"As shown in the picture..."` — breaks immersion immediately.
> - Memorising a pre-written story and awkwardly cramming the prompt's title into the last line (markers instantly penalise off-topic essays).
> - Shifting randomly between past tense (`walked`) and present tense (`walks`) mid-paragraph. Pick ONE primary tense and stay faithful to it.
> - Overusing thesaurus words (`"utilised my ocular organs"` instead of `"looked"`) — unnatural diction lowers your Language mark!

## Practice

```questions
Q: Why should you keep a narrative essay's timeframe tight (e.g. a single evening or conversation) rather than covering several years? (2)
A: A tight timeframe allows you to develop **vivid sensory detail, believable character tension, and controlled pacing** within 400–450 words, whereas spanning years forces rushed summary and shallow characterisation.
---
Q: In the DBE HL Paper 3 rubric, which criterion carries the largest share of the 50 marks, and what happens if a brilliant story ignores the prompt? (3)
A: **Content and Planning** carries **30 out of 50 marks (60%)**. Even fluent language cannot save an off-topic essay — failure to address the prompt directly drops Content into the lowest band and caps the overall mark.
---
Q: Identify the flaw in this visual-prompt opening and fix it in one sentence: *"Looking at this picture of an empty bus stop in the rain, it makes me think about loneliness."* (2)
A: **Flaw:** Never explicitly refer to `"this picture"` as an exam prompt; step directly inside the scene.
**Fix:** *"Rain hammered the rusted bus shelter long after the five-fifteen headlights had vanished around the bend."*
```
