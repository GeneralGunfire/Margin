> **What you'll learn:** how to decode a **literary essay prompt** (*"To what extent..."*, *"Critically discuss..."*), how to craft a sharp **thesis statement** instead of repeating the question, how to build disciplined **PEEL paragraphs** that never slip into plot summary, and how DBE examiners split the **25 marks** (Content 15 + Structure & Language 10).
>
> **Prerequisites:** character, theme, and textual evidence skills.
>
> **Why this matters:** A single literary essay is worth **25 out of 80 marks** in Paper 2 (and **25 out of 35 marks** in SBA Test 2!). Most learners lose 8–10 marks simply by **retelling the story** instead of building an argument anchored to a clear thesis!

## The idea in plain language

A literary essay is **NOT a book report**. Your teacher and the exam marker already know the plot of your novel, drama, or poem inside out! What they want to see is a **lawyer's argument**:
1. Take a clear, thoughtful position on the question (**Thesis**).
2. Prove that position across **3–4 focused body paragraphs**, using specific scenes, character actions, and short quotations as **evidence**, followed immediately by **analysis explaining HOW that evidence proves your thesis**!

### How the 25 marks are awarded (DBE HL Literary Essay Rubric)

- **Content (15 marks):** understanding of the text, **relevance to every part of the prompt**, depth of argument, and accuracy of textual proof/quotes. *(Off-topic plot summary caps Content very low, even if your English is fluent!)*
- **Structure & Language (10 marks):** clear introduction with thesis, logical paragraph flow with transitions, formal academic register (no slang/contractions!), **present literary tense**, and a crisp conclusion.

## Step 1: Unpacking the essay prompt & writing a thesis

Before writing a single sentence of your essay, break the prompt down into **3 checks**:
1. **The instruction phrase:**
   - *"Discuss..."* $\implies$ explain and analyse with proof.
   - *"To what extent do you agree..."* $\implies$ take a nuanced stance (*fully*, *partially*, or *largely agree — while noting an important qualification*), NOT a blind 50-50 fence-sit!
   - *"Critically evaluate / Assess..."* $\implies$ judge strengths, flaws, motives, and consequences.
2. **The key nouns/concepts:** highlight EVERY required element! If a question asks about *"how **pride** AND **miscommunication** destroy the relationship"*, discussing *only* pride leaves half the prompt unanswered!
3. **Your Thesis Statement (last 1–2 sentences of your Introduction):** a sharp claim stating **WHAT you argue** and **THE MAIN REASONS WHY** (your roadmap for the body paragraphs).

```compare
title: Weak Introduction (plot summary) vs Strong Introduction (Thesis-driven)
left: Weak (caps Content marks!)
*"The novel tells the story of a family living on a farm during a harsh drought. In this story, the father and son argue a lot. I will discuss whether pride causes conflict in this book."*
- Never use *"In this essay I will write about..."*!
- Wastes lines retelling setting instead of taking a stance.
right: Strong (hits top rubric band)
*"Although harsh economic hardship strains every relationship in the text, the family's fracture is driven primarily by stubborn pride rather than poverty alone. Both the father's refusal to accept advice and the son's secretive defiance turn soluble disagreements into irreversible estrangement."*
- Takes an explicit **stance** (`primarily... rather than...`).
- Previews **two/three concrete pillars** that become Body Paragraphs 1, 2, 3!
```

## Step 2: Building every body paragraph with PEEL

Every body paragraph defends **ONE single clear idea** using **PEEL**:

```steps
title: Inside one high-scoring PEEL paragraph
step: **P — Point (Topic Sentence):** state ONE specific argument advancing your thesis — NEVER start a paragraph with *"Next in Chapter 4..."*!
step: **E — Evidence:** support that point with a specific plot event, character choice, or **short woven quotation** (e.g. *When he dismisses the warning as `"mere superstition"`, ...*). Do NOT drop huge 4-line block quotes!
step: **E — Explain / Analyse:** the most important part! Unpack *why* that action or wording proves your point (motives, consequences, contrast, or author's technique). Aim for **2–3 sentences of explanation** for every 1 sentence of evidence!
step: **L — Link:** echo the essay prompt's key terms cleanly at the end of the paragraph to prove you stayed strictly on topic.
```

### Crucial grammar rule: Literary Present Tense & Formal Register

- **Write in the PRESENT TENSE** when discussing events inside literature (*"Macbeth **fears** Banquo"*, *"The speaker **highlights** the contrast"*, NOT *"Macbeth feared..."*!). Use past tense only when referring back to something that occurred *before* another literary moment you're analysing (*"Because he has already ignored the warning..."*).
- **Zero contractions (`don't`, `isn't`, `won't`):** write `do not`, `is not`, `will not`.
- **Zero slang or first-person filler (`I think`, `In my opinion`, `basically`, `guys`).**

## Worked example (building one complete PEEL paragraph)

**Prompt:** *In many tragedies, fear of losing status pushes characters toward moral compromise. Discuss how a character's desire for status leads to poor choices.*

> **[Point]** Firstly, a desperate desire to protect public reputation often blinds a protagonist to genuine ethical danger. **[Evidence]** Early in the conflict, when confronted privately about falsified inspection records, the director chooses to silence his assistant rather than admit an honest clerical error, insisting that *"perception is half of leadership"*. **[Explain]** By prioritising outward appearance (`"perception"`) over truth, he treats integrity as disposable whenever his social standing feels threatened. That single cover-up forces him into deeper dishonesty later on, because exposing the original lie would now destroy the exact prestige he tried to guard. **[Link]** Thus, valuing status above accountability triggers the first irreversible moral compromise.

Notice the ratio: **zero aimless retelling**, concise embedded quote, deep focus on **why** and **consequence**, and a crisp final **Link**!

```tryit
hint: Ask: what **claim about responsibility** is that scene proving?
answer: **Example upgrade:** *"Furthermore, Character X deepens his own downfall by repeatedly rejecting loyal allies whenever pride clouds his judgement — most notably during his heated refusal to heed Y's warning in the town square."* (Notice: argument first, scene attached as evidence!)
```

> **Common mistakes**
>
> - Starting body paragraphs with chronological markers (*"Firstly in Chapter 1..."*, *"Then later..."*) — that traps you in **retelling the story**! Organise by **ideas/themes/motives**, not chapter numbers.
> - Dropping a floating quotation as its own sentence without embedding or explaining it (*Quote dumping*). Always weave quotes into your own grammar and analyse them immediately.
> - Adding brand-new plot facts inside the **Conclusion**. Your conclusion should briefly synthesise your thesis in fresh words — no new quotes or new scenes!

## Practice

```questions
Q: Read this prompt carefully: *"Compare how two characters respond differently to adversity, and discuss what this reveals about resilience."* How many main requirements must your thesis and body address? (3)
A: 1. Specific examples of **how Character A responds** to adversity.
2. Specific contrast of **how Character B responds differently** to the adversity.
3. Explicit analysis of **what that contrast reveals about the theme of resilience**.
---
Q: Why do DBE Paper 2 rubrics deduct marks when a learner writes *"In conclusion, I really enjoyed this book because it teaches us many lessons"*? (2)
A: It slips into **informal, personal filler** (`"I really enjoyed"`) and offers a **vague cliché** (`"many lessons"`) instead of a formal, analytical synthesis tied back to the essay's specific thesis and textual themes.
---
Q: Rewrite this sentence into correct **literary present tense** and **formal register**: *"When the hero saw he couldn't win, he gave up and said he didn't care anymore."* (2)
A: *"When the hero **realises** he **cannot** prevail, he **surrenders** and **claims** that he no longer **cares**."*
```
