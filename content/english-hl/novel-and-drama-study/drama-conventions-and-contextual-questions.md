> **What you'll learn:** how reading a **drama** differs from reading a novel (stage directions, dialogue, **soliloquy**, **aside**, **dramatic irony**, blocking), how to place any extract accurately **in context**, and the strict rules for answering **Paper 2 contextual questions** (including the classic *"If you were the stage director..."* question).
>
> **Prerequisites:** character, plot arc, tone, and textual evidence.
>
> **Why this matters:** in Paper 2, contextual extract questions carry **25 marks** (or 10 marks in SBA Test 2). Learners frequently lose marks by forgetting **what happens immediately before/after the extract**, ignoring **stage directions** printed in brackets/italics, or failing to give **exact actor instructions** in director questions!

## The idea in plain language

A novel whispers directly inside your head through a narrator's descriptions. A **play** has **no narrator**! Everything we discover must happen live on a stage through:
1. **What characters say** (dialogue, monologues, pauses, interruptions).
2. **How they move, sound, and occupy space** (**stage directions** in italics/brackets).
3. **What the audience knows that characters on stage do NOT know** (**dramatic irony**).

Whenever you read an extract from a play in Paper 2, never skip the stage directions in italics — markers treat them as goldmines for character motivation and tension!

### Essential drama terms to memorise

```definition
title: Soliloquy vs Monologue vs Aside
A **monologue** is a long speech spoken by one character **to other characters** on stage.
A **soliloquy** is a speech delivered by a character **alone on stage**, revealing their unfiltered private thoughts directly to the audience.
An **aside** is a brief remark whispered by a character **directly to the audience** (or one ally) that other characters on stage supposedly cannot hear.
```

```definition
title: Dramatic irony (crucial!)
**Dramatic irony** occurs when the **audience knows a crucial truth or danger** that one or more characters on stage do NOT know — making the character's words carry a painful or suspenseful double meaning.
```

## Mastering the 5 classic Paper 2 contextual questions

No matter which novel or play your school studies, Paper 2 contextual questions follow five predictable templates:

### 1. "Place this extract in context." (2–3 marks)
- **What it asks:** What happens **immediately BEFORE** this moment in the plot (and/or what triggers this scene)?
- **Rule:** Do **not** retell the entire novel from Chapter 1! Name the **exact preceding event** that led straight into this conversation or confrontation.

### 2. "Account for [Character X]'s reaction / state of mind in line 14." (2–3 marks)
- **What it asks:** **Why** do they react this way, connecting what is happening *right here* to their backstory, secret fear, or earlier events in the text.

### 3. "Discuss the dramatic irony in lines 8–10." (2–3 marks)
- **Three-part rule for full marks:**
  1. State what the **character believes / says**.
  2. State what the **audience actually knows** from earlier scenes.
  3. Explain the **effect** (creates suspense, dread, pity, or exposes naivety/hypocrisy).

### 4. "If you were the director of this play, how would you direct [Actor] to deliver line 12? Mention BOTH tone of voice and body language." (2–3 marks)
- **Rule:** Put yourself in the director's chair! Give **two concrete stage instructions**:
  1. **Vocal tone / pace / volume** (e.g. *"quiet, strained whisper, pausing before the final word"* — NEVER just `"sadly"`!).
  2. **Physical movement / facial expression / gesture** (e.g. *"step back slightly, avoid eye contact, and clench the letter tightly against her chest"*).
  3. **Link why** (e.g. *"to convey her suppressed panic and fear of being overheard"*).

### 5. "Comment on the contrast between X in this extract and X earlier/later in the text." (3–4 marks)
- Always give **BOTH** halves: describe X's behaviour/attitude **earlier** (with detail) $\longrightarrow$ show **how and why** X has changed (or stayed hypocritically unchanged) **now**.

## Worked example

Read this short original drama extract:

> **THANDI:** *(pacing rapidly near the door, glancing at her wristwatch)* Have you packed the ledger? My brother arrives in ten minutes.
> **MARCUS:** *(calmly pouring tea, not looking up)* Relax, Thandi. Nobody checks the back office on a Friday.
> **THANDI:** You never take anything seriously! *(freezes as heavy footsteps echo in the hallway)*

**Sample contextual questions & model answers:**

1. **Explain how the stage directions reveal the contrast between Thandi and Marcus here. (2 marks)**
   - **Answer:** Thandi's `"pacing rapidly"` and repeated glances at her watch reveal **nervousness and urgency** (1), contrasting sharply with Marcus `"calmly pouring tea"` and refusing to look up, which reveals his **complacent, dismissive attitude** toward the danger (1).
2. **Suggest how the actor playing Thandi should deliver `"You never take anything seriously!"` in terms of tone and body language. (3 marks)**
   - **Answer:** She should speak in a **sharp, rising, exasperated tone** (1), **halting her pacing to turn squarely toward Marcus with hands raised/pointing accusingly** (1), showing how Marcus's calm indifference pushes her anxiety into frustration just before the footsteps interrupt (1).
3. **If the audience already saw Thandi's brother lock the front entrance five minutes ago, identify the literary device created by Marcus's line (`"Nobody checks the back office on a Friday"`) and explain its effect. (2 marks)**
   - **Answer:** **Dramatic irony** (1). The audience knows Thandi's brother is already inside/trapping them while Marcus smugly assumes they are completely safe, raising **tension and dread** for what will happen next (1).

```tryit
title: Spot the missing half in this "Place in context" answer
prompt: Question: **Place this extract in context. (2 marks)**
Student wrote: *"Thandi and Marcus are talking in an office."*
Why does this score **0 marks**?
hint: Does stating where they are standing explain *what event just happened before this scene*?
answer: Simply describing the visible setting earns **0**! `"Place in context"` means explaining the **preceding plot events** that caused this exact scene (e.g. *"Moments earlier, Marcus concealed missing funds/stole the ledger, and Thandi has just discovered her brother is returning unexpectedly to audit the office"*).
```

> **Common mistakes**
>
> - Writing `"sad tone"` or `"angry expression"` in a director question without specifying concrete **actions, posture, eye contact, and vocal delivery**!
> - Skipping stage directions printed in italics — treat every stage direction as direct exam evidence!
> - Summarising the whole play from Act 1 Scene 1 on a 2-mark `"Place this extract in context"` question instead of focusing tightly on the event immediately leading into the extract.

## Practice

```questions
Q: Why does a playwright include a **soliloquy** in a tragedy or tense drama? (2)
A: Because a play has no narrator, a soliloquy lets the character speak aloud alone on stage to **reveal hidden motives, inner conflict, or secret plans directly to the audience** (1), often building dramatic irony or empathy (1).
---
Q: Distinguish between a **comedy**, a **tragedy**, and **tragicomedy** in broad dramatic terms. (3)
A: A **tragedy** deals with serious themes where a protagonist faces downfall or death (often through a tragic flaw or fate); a **comedy** uses humour, misunderstandings, and satire towards a harmonious ending; **tragicomedy** blends dark/painful realities with dark humour or bittersweet hope.
---
Q: In a stage direction: `[Turns sharply away from the window, voice dropping to a whisper]`. State what TWO theatrical elements are specified here and what mood they create. (3)
A: Physical movement/blocking (`turns sharply away`) (1) and vocal volume/delivery (`voice dropping to a whisper`) (1), creating a **tense, secretive, or fearful** mood suggesting danger outside (1).
```
