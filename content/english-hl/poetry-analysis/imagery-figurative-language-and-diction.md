> **What you'll learn:** how to identify every major **figure of speech** in Grade 11 seen and unseen poetry (comparisons, contradictions, exaggerations), why **simply naming a metaphor earns at most 1 out of 3 marks**, and how to unpack imagery using the **Image $\to$ Meaning $\to$ Effect** method.
>
> **Prerequisites:** denotation, connotation, and literal vs figurative language.
>
> **Why this matters:** Paper 2 Section A Poetry (30 marks: 20 seen + 10 unseen) asks *"Comment on the effectiveness of the imagery in line 6"* in almost every poem. Unpacking **what is compared to what** and **why** turns 1/3 answers into 3/3!

## The idea in plain language

Why don't poets write *"I felt lonely in the big city"*? Because plain statements inform your brain without letting you *feel* the moment. When a poet writes *"Neon towers swallowed the tiny shadows on the pavement"*, your mind instantly sees towering buildings as hungry monsters devouring small, helpless people!

Every poetic image works like an **equals-with-a-twist** linking two different worlds:
1. The **literal thing** in the poem (the lonely city streets).
2. The **figurative picture** borrowed from elsewhere (hungry monsters swallowing tiny prey).

The marks in Paper 2 lie in explaining **the exact point of similarity** between those two worlds!

### Key figures of speech grouped by family

#### Family 1: Comparison devices (linking two unlike things)
- **Simile:** explicit comparison using **`like`** or **`as`** (*"Memories lingered **like** woodsmoke in winter"*).
- **Metaphor:** direct implicit comparison stating A **is** B (*"Hope is a stubborn green shoot breaking tarmac"*).
- **Extended metaphor:** a single metaphor developed across several lines or an entire poem!
- **Personification:** giving **human qualities, emotions, or actions** to something non-human or abstract (*"The wind **whispered** warnings through the rafters"*).

#### Family 2: Contradiction & contrast devices
- **Oxymoron:** **two opposite words placed right next to each other** (*"deafening silence"*, *"bittersweet farewell"*, *"living death"*).
- **Paradox:** a whole **statement** that seems absurd or contradictory at first, yet reveals a deeper truth (*"I must be cruel only to be kind"*).
- **Antithesis / Juxtaposition:** placing contrasting ideas side by side inside balanced phrasing (*"To err is human; to forgive, divine"*).
- **Irony:** contrast between what is expected/said and what is actually meant or happens.

#### Family 3: Substitution, exaggeration & understatement
- **Hyperbole:** deliberate exaggeration for emphasis or humour (*"A thousand oceans separate our desks"*).
- **Litotes / Understatement:** deliberately downplaying something huge (often with a negative: *"Not bad at all"* to mean brilliant).
- **Metonymy & Synecdoche:** substituting a closely linked attribute (*"the Crown"* for monarchy — **metonymy**) or a specific part representing the whole (*"all hands on deck"* for sailors — **synecdoche**).
- **Apostrophe:** directly addressing an absent person, dead hero, or lifeless object (*"O Death, where is thy sting?"*).

## The 3-mark Paper 2 imagery formula (IME)

Never stop after naming the figure of speech! Use **I – M – E**:

```steps
title: Answering "Discuss the effectiveness of the imagery" (3 marks)
step: **I — Identify:** name the device accurately and quote the short key phrase (e.g. *Metaphor: `"stubborn green shoot"`*).
step: **M — Meaning (both halves):** name the two things being compared (`hope` compared to a `young plant pushing through concrete`) and state the **shared quality** (`fragile yet unstoppable persistence against harsh odds`).
step: **E — Effect:** explain what this reveals about the speaker's **mood, theme, or attitude** (*emphasises resilient optimism despite bleak hardship*).
```

## Worked example

Consider these four original lines of unseen poetry:

> *"Time is a quiet thief with velvet shoes,
> lifting the bright coins from our pockets while we argue over clocks;
> only at twilight do we notice the light has grown thin,
> wearing away like an old woollen coat."*

**Questions & model 3-mark answers:**

1. **Identify and discuss the effectiveness of the metaphor in lines 1–2 (`"Time is a quiet thief... lifting the bright coins"`). (3 marks)**
   - **Answer:** **Metaphor** (and personification) (1): Time is compared to a silent thief stealing `"bright coins"` (our valuable days/youth) while we are distracted `"arguing over clocks"` (wasting time on trivial schedules) (1). The comparison is effective because `"velvet shoes"` emphasises how silently and invisibly life slips away before we value it (1).
2. **Comment on the simile in the final line (`"wearing away like an old woollen coat"`). (2 marks)**
   - **Answer:** Sunlight/youth is compared via `"like"` to a worn woollen garment thinning over years of use (1), conveying gradual, inevitable ageing and fading warmth as evening/old age nears (1).

```tryit
hint: Unpack **WHY** *"wet iron"* specifically — what properties do waterlogged metal and heavy grief share?
answer: Saying *"creates a clear image"* scores **0**! Fix: *Simile (1): grief is compared to `"wet iron"` — something cold, dense, suffocating, and impossible to lift off easily (1) — highlighting how crushing and inescapable her sorrow feels emotionally and physically (1).*
```

> **Common mistakes**
>
> - Calling a **metaphor** a **simile** (or vice versa) — always scan for `like` / `as` (and make sure `like` is actually comparing two different things, not `"I like coffee"`!).
> - Confusing **oxymoron** (two adjacent words: *"icy fire"*) with **paradox** (a full apparent contradiction sentence: *"Less is more"*).
> - Leaving out **what two things are being compared** and jumping straight to vague praise (*"shows the theme well"*).

## Practice

```questions
Q: Identify the figure of speech in each line and briefly explain its effect:
(a) *"The ancient oak stretched its gnarled arms to catch the dawn."*
(b) *"In the crowded stadium, a terrible silence screamed."*
A: (a) **Personification** (1): the tree is given human `"arms"` reaching intentionally to `"catch"` light, making nature appear alive and welcoming (1).
(b) **Oxymoron + personification** (`"terrible silence screamed"`): pairs silence with screaming to convey how shocking and overwhelming the sudden quiet felt (2).
---
Q: What is the difference between a **symbol** and a **metaphor** in poetry? (2)
A: A **metaphor** equates two unlike things figuratively in a line (*"Life is a highway"*), whereas a **symbol** is an actual literal object in the poem (e.g. a wall, a broken mirror, a rising sun) that also represents a larger abstract idea throughout the poem.
---
Q: Distinguish between **irony** and **pun** in one clear pair of definitions. (2)
A: **Irony** highlights a contradiction between expectation/words and reality; a **pun** plays on a single word's two meanings or two similar-sounding words for wit or emphasis.
```
