> **What you'll learn:** how poets use **sound devices** (alliteration, assonance, onomatopoeia, sibilance, rhythm) to echo physical action, how **structure and form** (sonnets, stanzas, enjambment, caesura, volta) control pace and argument, and how to write high-scoring answers on **poetic tone and mood**.
>
> **Prerequisites:** figures of speech and comprehension tone vocabulary.
>
> **Why this matters:** learners often ignore stanza breaks, line endings, and sound patterns — yet Paper 2 poems (especially sonnets and modern free verse) always allocate 3–5 marks directly to **structure, enjambment, sound, and shifts in tone**!

## The idea in plain language

Poetry is meant to be **heard** as well as read. Even before you translate a poem's metaphors, the **rhythm, line breaks, and repeated sounds** act like a film soundtrack:
- Fast, clipped consonants (`t, k, p`) sound sharp, frantic, or violent.
- Long vowel sounds (`oo, ah, oh`) slow the voice down, sounding mournful, dreamy, or weary.
- Soft `s` and `sh` sounds (**sibilance**) whisper like wind, waves, or secrets.
- A line spilling smoothly without punctuation over the edge into the next line (**enjambment**) speeds the pace up like rushing water — whereas a sharp punctuation stop right in the middle of a line (**caesura**) slams on the brakes!

### Definitions to memorise

```definition
title: Alliteration vs Assonance vs Onomatopoeia
**Alliteration** is the repetition of **initial consonant sounds** in nearby words (*"cold, crisp currents"*).
**Assonance** is the repetition of **vowel sounds** inside nearby words (*"slowly floating home"*).
**Onomatopoeia** uses words whose spoken sound **imitates** the real-world sound (*hiss, crackle, boom, rustle*).
```

```definition
title: Enjambment vs Caesura
**Enjambment (run-on line)** occurs when a sentence or clause continues smoothly past the end of a poetic line into the next line with **no end punctuation**, speeding up rhythm or mirroring uninterrupted flow.
**Caesura** is a strong deliberate pause inside a poetic line (via comma, dash, colon, or full stop) that slows rhythm or highlights a dramatic shift.
```

## Poetic structure & form essentials (Grade 11–12)

Whenever a Paper 2 question says *"Comment on the structure of the poem"*, check **all four** of these features:

1. **Fixed form vs Free verse:**
   - **Shakespearean / English Sonnet:** 14 lines = **3 quatrains** (4 lines each, rhyme `ABAB CDCD EFEF` developing an argument/problem) + **1 rhyming couplet** (`GG`, delivering a twist, summary, or punchline!).
   - **Petrarchan / Italian Sonnet:** 14 lines = **octave** (8 lines, `ABBAABBA` presenting problem/question) $\longrightarrow$ **volta** (turn around line 9!) $\longrightarrow$ **sestet** (6 lines, `CDECDE` / `CDCDCD` giving resolution or shift!).
   - **Free verse:** irregular line lengths and no fixed rhyme scheme — mirrors natural speech, chaos, freedom, or raw emotion!
2. **Stanza layout & single-line emphasis:** why did the poet isolate one short stanza or a single two-word line on its own? Usually for stark emphasis, isolation, or shock!
3. **Shift / Volta:** watch for turning-point words like **`But`**, **`Yet`**, **`However`**, **`Now`** — they almost always signal a **shift in tone**!
4. **Rhyme scheme:** label matching end sounds with letters (`ABAB` etc.). Regular rhyme creates harmony, predictability, or song-like musicality; broken rhyme jars the reader to reflect discord or grief.

## Connecting Sound & Structure to Meaning (never say "it gives rhythm"!)

Paper 2 memos **reject** generic answers like *"Alliteration creates rhythm"* or *"Enjambment makes the poem flow nicely"*. Always tie the sound/structure directly to **what is happening in that specific line**:

| Device | Weak 0-mark answer | Strong 2-mark Paper 2 answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Alliteration** (`"bitter blades bite"`) | *"Creates musical rhythm and makes it catchy."* | *"Harsh plosive `b`-alliteration mimics the sharp, violent sting of winter frost, reinforcing the speaker's hostility toward the cold."* |
| **Sibilance** (`"softly sighing sea"`) | *"Uses s-sounds for nice sound effect."* | *"Repeated soft `s`-sounds imitate the gentle whispering wash of waves, creating a calm, soothing mood."* |
| **Enjambment** | *"Makes the line flow smoothly."* | *"The run-on line mirrors the bird's continuous, unbroken swoop through the sky without pausing."* |

## Worked example

Examine this short stanza about a runner approaching the finish line:

> *"Lungs burn; legs lock — yet forward spills the stride
> past roaring crowds and blurred white chalk lines
> until the tape snaps clean across the chest."*

**Sample Paper 2 questions & answers:**

1. **Comment on how the punctuation in line 1 (`"Lungs burn; legs lock —"`) affects the pace and reflects the runner's state. (2 marks)**
   - **Answer:** The semicolon and dash act as **caesuras** breaking line 1 into short, breathless bursts (1), mimicking the runner's painful gasps and physical exhaustion before the sudden surge after `"yet"` (1).
2. **Explain the effect of the enjambment between lines 1 and 2 (`"spills the stride / past roaring crowds"`). (2 marks)**
   - **Answer:** The lack of punctuation at the end of line 1 forces the reader to rush seamlessly into line 2 (1), mirroring the unstoppable momentum of the runner surging past the spectators (1).
3. **Explain the change in tone from the beginning of line 1 to line 3. (2 marks)**
   - **Answer:** The tone shifts from **strained, agonising determination** in `"Lungs burn; legs lock"` (1) to **decisive triumph and relief** as `"the tape snaps clean"` at the finish (1).

```tryit
title: Sonnet spotting check
prompt: A 14-line poem spends lines 1–8 describing deep despair over a lost homeland (`ABBAABBA`). At line 9, the first word is `"Yet"` and the remaining 6 lines celebrate enduring cultural memory (`CDECDE`).
Name the **type of sonnet** and the technical name for the turn at line 9!
hint: Compare `8 + 6` (Octave + Sestet) vs `4 + 4 + 4 + 2`.
answer: It is a **Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet** (`ABBAABBA` octave + `CDECDE` sestet), and the turning point at line 9 signalled by `"Yet"` is the **volta**!
```

> **Common mistakes**
>
> - Matching **letters** instead of **SOUNDS** for alliteration/assonance (`"city cat"` is NOT alliteration — `/s/` vs `/k/`! `"know now"` IS alliteration — both start with `/n/`!).
> - Giving generic answers (*"makes the poem sound musical"* or *"helps the reader remember"*) without linking to the **scene, mood, or meaning**.
> - Reporting only ONE tone when a question asks how tone **changes** across a poem — always name **Tone A (early, with quote)** $\longrightarrow$ **Tone B (later, with quote)**!

## Practice

```questions
Q: Identify TWO sound devices in `"Hushed shadows shimmered over silver stones"` and explain their combined mood. (3)
A: **Sibilance** (repetition of soft `s`/`sh` consonant sounds in *hushed, shadows, shimmered, silver, stones*) (1) and **alliteration** (`s` in *silver stones* / `sh` in *shadows shimmered*) (1). Together they create a quiet, mysterious, peaceful night-time atmosphere (1).
---
Q: What is the difference between a **Shakespearean sonnet** and a **Petrarchan sonnet** in structure? (2)
A: A Shakespearean sonnet consists of **three quatrains and a final rhyming couplet** (`ABAB CDCD EFEF GG`), whereas a Petrarchan sonnet consists of an **octave (8 lines) followed by a sestet (6 lines)** with a volta around line 9.
---
Q: Explain the difference between **rhythm** and **rhyme**. (2)
A: **Rhythm** is the pattern of stressed and unstressed beats/pace across a line, whereas **rhyme** is the matching of identical or similar **end sounds** (usually at line endings).
```
